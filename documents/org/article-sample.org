#+BEGIN_QUOTE
A ferry is a bridge that admits it is expensive.

--- attributed to an unnamed transport planner, probably apocryphal
#+END_QUOTE

For forty years the answer to a river was a bridge, and the answer to a bridge
was a bigger bridge. Then, quietly, in a dozen coastal districts at once, the
ferries came back. This is a fictional piece of reporting written as a sample
document; the districts, the operators, and the figures in it are invented.

* A service nobody planned to restart

  The Northwind Ferry Cooperative did not set out to run four routes. It set out
  to run one, for a summer, while the Halloway bridge was resurfaced. The
  resurfacing took eleven weeks. The ferry has now run for six years.

  What happened in those eleven weeks is the whole story in miniature. Commuters
  who had never considered the water discovered a crossing that took nineteen
  minutes instead of forty. When the bridge reopened, ridership on the temporary
  ferry fell by a third --- and then stopped falling. The remaining two-thirds
  had not been waiting for the bridge. They had been waiting for the ferry,
  without knowing it.

* Three things had to be true

  Not every district that tried this succeeded. Comparing the ones that did
  against the ones that did not, the same three conditions keep appearing.

** The water had to be shorter than the road

   This sounds obvious and is routinely ignored. A ferry that crosses in twelve
   minutes still loses to a bridge if passengers spend twenty minutes reaching
   the terminal. The successful routes are the ones where the terminal is where
   people already are.

** The timetable had to be boring

   Every operator that published a clever timetable --- tidal adjustments,
   seasonal variants, different Sunday patterns --- watched ridership stall. The
   ones that ran every twenty minutes, all day, every day, did not. A service you
   have to consult is a service you use only when you have already decided to.

** Somebody had to own the last mile

   The single strongest predictor of a route's ridership, in the cooperative's
   own analysis, is whether the terminal has secure bicycle parking. Not the
   vessel. Not the fare. The bicycle racks.

* The argument about Halloway

  Halloway carries the fewest passengers, recovers the least of its cost, and is
  cancelled more often than any other route because it is the most exposed to
  weather. Every internal review recommends closing it. It is still running.

  The reason is that Halloway is the only route serving a settlement with no
  alternative crossing at all. Its passengers are not choosing the ferry over a
  bridge; they are choosing it over a fifty-minute drive around an estuary. That
  does not make the route cheaper. It makes the comparison a different one.

  #+BEGIN_QUOTE
  "We keep measuring Halloway against the Harbour Loop. It isn't the same kind
  of thing. One is a commuter service. The other is the road."
  #+END_QUOTE

* What would change the picture

  Three developments, none of them certain, would each move the numbers more
  than anything the cooperative can do on its own.

  1. *Electrification.* Two of the four vessels are due for replacement within
     six years. Battery-electric equivalents would cut fuel cost by roughly
     two-thirds on the shorter routes and would not work at all on the longest.
  2. *Terminal consolidation.* Three of the seven terminals are within eight
     hundred metres of another. Closing two would shorten every route that calls
     at them, at the cost of a longer walk for a minority of passengers.
  3. *A single fare with the buses.* Passengers currently pay twice. Every study
     of this in every district has found the same thing, which is that
     integrated fares raise ridership more than any timetable change ever has.

  None of the three is within the cooperative's authority.

* The thing the revival is not

  It is tempting to read the return of the municipal ferry as nostalgia, or as a
  verdict on bridges. It is neither. The routes that work are the ones where the
  geography was always in the water's favour and the road was a forty-year
  detour around admitting it. Where the bridge really is shorter, the ferry has
  not come back and will not.

  What the revival actually demonstrates is narrower and more useful: that a
  service can be uneconomic for forty years and economic in the forty-first,
  without anything about the water changing, because the cost of the alternative
  moved. The ferries did not get better. Driving got worse.
