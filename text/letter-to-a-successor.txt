Dear Tobias,

You will read this on your first morning, which is a strange time to be handed
a letter, so I will say at the outset that nothing in it is urgent and nothing
in it is bad news. Put it in a drawer and read it properly at the end of the
week if that suits you better.

I am writing because the handover we did last Thursday covered everything that
can be covered in four hours, and four hours is enough for the systems and the
suppliers and the passwords, and it is not enough for anything else. What
follows is the anything else. It is one person's opinion, thirty-one years
deep, and you should feel entirely free to disagree with all of it.


ON THE COLLECTION

The catalogue tells you what we hold. It does not tell you what we hold it
for, and in about a dozen cases the difference matters.

The Marlow Street bequest is the obvious one. Two hundred and twelve volumes,
accepted in 2025 on the condition that they be available to readers within two
years of receipt. That deadline is June. Conservation is under way and
Ashworth & Pell are reliable, but the schedule has no float in it, and if
anything slips you must tell the family before you tell the committee. They
gave us the books because the previous head told them we would do this
properly. That promise is now yours.

Item one hundred and six in that bequest has no title page, no colophon, and
an index in extremely bad Latin. Yolanda spent a fortnight on it and produced
a catalogue entry ending "the compiler's intent is not recoverable from the
object." You will be asked to change that sentence. Do not change it. It is
the most honest thing in the catalogue and the person asking has not read the
book.

The local history collection at Halloway Bank is legally ours and morally the
village's. Every ten years somebody proposes consolidating it into Central.
Every ten years the same four families object, and they are right, and the
proposal goes away for another decade. Yours will arrive in about three years.
Save yourself the trouble and decline it early.


ON THE STAFF

You know them all already, so I will restrict myself to the things that do not
come up in a handover.

Fenella has been conserving books for thirty years and is better at it than
anyone we could hire. She will tell you, roughly twice a year, that she is
thinking of retiring. She is not. She is telling you that something is
bothering her and has not found a way to raise it. Ask what it is. It has
never once been about retiring.

Yolanda is the best cataloguer I have worked with and she will leave within
two years, because we cannot pay her what she is worth and because she knows
it and because she is too polite to say so. When she goes, do not try to
replace her with one person. It will take two.

Aurelio will do anything you ask and will not tell you when it is too much.
This is not modesty; it is how he was trained, forty years ago, in a service
that no longer exists. You will have to notice on his behalf. I got this wrong
twice and both times it cost him a month.

There is a rota argument every February about the Easter opening. It is not
really about Easter. It is about the fact that the same two people always
cover the awkward weeks and everybody knows it and nobody will say it in a
meeting. You could fix this in an afternoon by writing it down. I never did,
and I am not entirely sure why not.


ON THE COMMITTEE

They are, with one exception, well-intentioned people who read the papers on
the train. Write the papers accordingly: the recommendation first, the
reasoning second, the tables at the back.

Never put a number in a paper you cannot defend from memory. You will be asked
about exactly one number, it will always be the one you took on trust from
somebody else, and you will be asked about it in front of the press.

The exception is Councillor Pell, who reads everything, remembers everything,
and is against most of it. He is not your enemy. He is the only person in the
room who will tell you why a proposal is weak before it is public, and if you
can get him to object in private you will save yourself a year. Take him the
difficult paper first. He will be surprised the first time and expect it
thereafter.

The opening-hours argument is the one that matters and it is the one you will
lose most often. We published the analysis in 2025: hours predict borrowing
far better than stock does. Nobody has ever challenged it and nobody has ever
acted on it, because hours are staff and staff are the budget line that moves.
Keep making the argument anyway. Make it every year, in the same words, so
that when somebody finally asks why we did not warn them, the answer is in
eight separate minutes.


ON THE BUILDING

The floor void in the east wing contains services that are on no drawing the
council holds. We discovered this in 2011 and again in 2027, at a cost of
seven weeks the second time, because the file from 2011 was in a cabinet
nobody thought to open.

I have put that file on your desk. Please put it somewhere your successor will
find it, and please tell them where it is in a letter like this one, because
the filing system will not.

The roof over the reading room is nineteen years old and rated for
twenty-five. Do not let anybody tell you that means six years. It means
somebody should look at it now.


ON THE JOB

Three things I wish somebody had told me.

The first is that most of what you do will be invisible, and that the visible
part will be the part you did least. You will spend eleven months on a stock
policy that nobody reads and one afternoon on a photograph for the newspaper,
and the photograph is what people will mention.

The second is that the service is not the buildings and it is not the
collection. It is the fact that at half past nine on a Tuesday morning
somebody unlocks a door and a person who has nowhere else to be comes in and
sits down. Everything else is in support of that. When a decision is
difficult, ask which option keeps more doors open on more Tuesdays, and take
it. That test has never once let me down and I only worked it out in about
year nineteen.

The third is that you will be told, at some point, that the service is
declining, that reading is finished, that the internet has settled the
question. You will be told this by people who have not been in a branch since
they were at school. The figures do not support it, the queue at Central at
half past nine does not support it, and you should not spend a single evening
worrying about it. Spend the evenings on the roof instead.


I am at the Cooper Street house from October and the number has not changed.
Ring me about anything except the things you are perfectly capable of deciding
yourself, which is most of them.

It has been the great privilege of my working life to hold this post. I am
glad it is you.

With every good wish,

Marguerite Oyelaran
