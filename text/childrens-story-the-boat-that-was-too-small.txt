THE BOAT THAT WAS TOO SMALL

A story for reading aloud


There was once a boat called Pip, and Pip was too small.

Everybody said so. The big ferry said so, and the fishing boats said so, and
even the harbour crane said so, and the harbour crane hardly said anything at
all.

"You are too small," said the big ferry, whose name was Marlow and who carried
three hundred and eighty people. "You could not carry three hundred and eighty
people."

"I could carry six," said Pip.

"Six," said Marlow. "Hmph."

And Marlow went off across the water with three hundred and eighty people on
board, and every one of them waved, and Pip sat at the end of the quay with
nobody on board at all.


Now Pip had a job, but it was a very quiet job.

Every morning, Pip took Old Ada across to the island to feed the goats. And
every afternoon, Pip brought Old Ada back again.

That was the whole job. One person, twice a day, and the goats did not even
come to the quay to say thank you.

"It is not much of a job," said the fishing boats.

"It is Ada's job," said Pip.

The fishing boats did not know what to say to that, so they went out to sea
instead, which is what fishing boats do when they do not know what to say.


One day the wind came.

It came from the north-east and it came all at once, and by breakfast the
water in the harbour was standing up on its back legs.

"I am not going out in that," said the big ferry Marlow.

"Nor am I," said the fishing boats.

"Nor am I," said the harbour crane, who was not going out in anything ever,
but who liked to join in.

And they all looked at Pip.

Pip looked at the water. It was very grey. It was much bigger than Pip, and it
was doing something complicated with itself over by the harbour mouth.

"Ada is on the island," said Pip.

"Ada will be perfectly all right," said Marlow. "There is a hut. There are
goats."

"Ada has her tablets in her coat," said Pip, "and her coat is on the quay."

And everybody was very quiet, because that was a different sort of thing
altogether.


Then Pip said the thing that Pip had been thinking for a long time and had
never said out loud.

"I cannot go out in that," said Pip. "I am too small. I would not get
halfway."

"Well," said Marlow. "There you are, then."

"But I know where the shallow water is," said Pip.

Everybody looked at Pip again.

"There is a way round," said Pip, "along the sand, where the water is only up
to here." (Pip meant the green line, which is the line all boats have, though
nobody talks about it.) "It is three times as far. It takes an hour instead of
eleven minutes. But the wind cannot get at you, because the sandbank is in the
way."

"That is not a route," said Marlow. "That is not on the chart."

"It is not on your chart," said Pip.

And Pip untied, very carefully, and went.


The way round the sandbank was long and it was slow and it was extremely dull,
which Pip had not expected. Pip had expected it to be frightening.

But the sandbank did exactly what sandbanks do, which is sit there, and the
wind went over the top of Pip and made a great deal of noise about it and
could not reach the water at all.

Pip went past the mussel poles. Pip went past the marker with the crooked top.
Pip went past the place where the seals sleep, and one seal opened one eye and
shut it again, because a small boat going slowly is not worth waking up for.

And after fifty-one minutes, Pip came round the end of the sandbank into the
little bay on the island, where the water was flat and green.

Ada was on the jetty with her arms folded.

"You took your time," said Ada.

"I came the long way," said Pip.

"I should hope so," said Ada. "I'd have been very cross if you'd come the
short way in this."

And she got in, and she put on her coat, which Pip had brought, and she took
her tablets, and she did not make a fuss about it, because Ada had been
getting into small boats for sixty-one years and had used up all her fuss a
long time ago.


They came back the long way too.

When they came into the harbour, Marlow was still tied up, and the fishing
boats were still tied up, and the harbour crane was still exactly where the
harbour crane had always been.

"You went," said Marlow.

"I went the long way," said Pip.

"Fifty-one minutes," said Marlow. "I can do it in eleven."

"Yes," said Pip. "Today you could do it in none."

And Marlow thought about this for quite a while, which is a thing big ferries
are not very good at, and then Marlow said:

"Tell me about the sandbank."

So Pip did.


After that, things were a little different, but not very.

Pip still took Ada over in the morning and brought her back in the afternoon.
Marlow still carried three hundred and eighty people and everybody still
waved. The harbour crane still hardly said anything.

But when the wind came from the north-east, everybody looked at Pip, and Pip
said either "yes" or "no," and whichever Pip said, that was what happened.

And once, in November, Marlow asked Pip to come out alongside and show the way
past the mussel poles, and Pip did, very slowly, with Marlow following behind
like an enormous careful dog.

"You are still too small," said Marlow afterwards.

"Yes," said Pip.

"That was not a complaint," said Marlow.

"No," said Pip. "I know."


THE END


A note for whoever is reading this aloud: Pip's route round the sandbank is
invented, and so are Pip, Ada, Marlow, the goats, and the harbour. If a small
listener asks whether the seal woke up, the answer is that it did, eventually,
about an hour later, and was annoyed about it.
