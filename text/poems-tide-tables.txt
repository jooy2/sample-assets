TIDE TABLES

Poems

Oswin Bramble


                          I. WATER


TIDE TABLE

The book says the water will be here at four.
The water does not read.
It arrives at four.

This is the oldest agreement
between a thing that keeps promises
and a thing that writes them down.


ESTUARY, FEBRUARY

Mud, and the smell of mud,
and a heron standing in it
like a decision nobody has made yet.

Somewhere upriver a gate opens.
The heron does not look up.
It has been standing here

since before the gate,
since before the river had a name
that could be written on a gate.


FERRY, LAST SAILING

Six of us and a bicycle.
The engine finds its note
and holds it, the way a held note

stops being sound
and becomes a room.
Nobody speaks.

Not from unfriendliness —
from the ordinary courtesy
of people who will be strangers again

in eleven minutes.


WHAT THE WATER DOES NOT DO

It does not rise to meet you.
It does not remember the boat.
It does not, whatever the poem says,

grieve, or wait, or forgive.

It goes where the moon and the land
have already agreed it will go,
and this is the whole of its kindness:

that it is never, once,
about you.


SPRING TIDE

Twice a month the water forgets
where it agreed to stop
and the road becomes a suggestion.

The town has known this for four hundred years
and builds there anyway,
which is either faith or arithmetic

and has been called both
by people who were equally sure.


                         II. WORK


THE LAMPLIGHTER

Ninety-four steps and a brass key.
An hour and forty minutes
of a job that ends

the moment anyone invents
a better way to do it.
She knew this the whole time.

She climbed anyway.
Not out of protest —
out of the evening being the evening

and the lamp being unlit.


NIGHT SHIFT

The building at three in the morning
is a different building.
The lift takes longer.

The corridors have opinions.

You learn which floors hum
and which ones only hum on Tuesdays,
and you tell nobody,

because there is nobody
and because it would not survive the telling.


HANDOVER

Everything I know about this machine
fits in forty minutes
if you already know machines,

and does not fit at all
if you do not.

So: forty minutes,
a diagram I drew badly,
and the number of the man

who wrote it in 1998
and does not answer.


THE INVENTORY

Fourth count, fourth answer.
The problem was never arithmetic.

Behind the mooring rope,
third shelf, on no list since 1994:
forty-one brass keys,

each tagged,
each tag naming a lamp post
that no longer stands.

I counted them twice.
Forty-one both times —
the first thing all week

that counted correctly.
I put the box back
and wrote nothing in the ledger

and the fifth count came out right.


RETIREMENT, SECOND WEEK

The alarm goes off out of habit.
He lets it.

Some mornings a thing is worth keeping
precisely because it is no longer
worth anything.


                       III. WEATHER


FOG, HALLOWAY

The far shore is a rumour.
The near shore is a rumour
the moment you turn your back on it.

Somewhere a bell,
and the bell is honest,
and the bell is the only honest thing

for a mile in any direction.


WIND, FORCE SEVEN

Everything that can be blown away
has been.

What is left is the argument
the coast has been making
for eleven thousand years,

at volume,
to nobody.


RAIN ON A METAL ROOF

Not a drumming.

A conversation
conducted entirely in one syllable
by an enormous number of speakers

who have agreed
not to interrupt.


FIRST FROST

The grass has been issued
with an opinion overnight.

By ten it will have withdrawn it.
By eleven it will deny
having held it.

Only the shadowed side of the wall
keeps the record.


THE FORECAST

Eighty per cent, they said,
which is a number about them
and not about the sky.

The sky does not deal in percentages.
It deals in one of two things
and has already decided which.


                        IV. ROOMS


READING ROOM, MARLOW STREET

Eleven people, none of them together,
all of them companionable.

The radiator ticks.
A page turns three seats down
and everybody, without looking,

knows which seat.


KITCHEN, AFTER

The soup was the announcement.
The argument was the soup's
and it lost.

We ate it anyway.

That is what a sister is:
someone whose losing argument
you finish the whole bowl of.


EMPTY FLAT, VIEWING

Somebody measured this room
against a life
and the life did not fit

or fitted and ended.

The agent says it has potential.
So did they.

We are all of us
previous occupants.


THE DRAWER

A key to a lock
on a post
that no longer has a lamp

in a town that no longer
needs anybody to climb it.

Council property.
Sixty years.
Nobody ever asked.


LAST ROOM

Turn the light off from the door
and the room becomes what it was
before anybody furnished it:

a volume of air
with a window in one wall
and no opinion about who leaves.


                         NOTES


These poems were written as a sample asset, in one sitting, for a repository
that needed a long plain-text file with verse in it. They are not previously
published and there is no poet called Oswin Bramble.

The place names — Corrance, Fenwick, Halloway, Marlow Street, Fisher's Rise —
are shared with the other fiction in this collection, on the principle that a
sample library may as well have a consistent geography. None of those places
exists.

Line breaks and stanza breaks are meaningful and are preserved with ordinary
newlines rather than any markup, which is the point of a plain-text sample: a
tool that reflows this file has damaged it, and a tool that does not has
passed.
