REMARKS AT THE OPENING OF THE NORTH LANDING TERMINAL

Delivered by the Chair of the Northwind Ferry Cooperative North Landing, 2 September 2027

As delivered


Thank you.
And thank you all for coming out in this, which the forecast promised would be a bright morning and which has turned out to be what we usually get.

I have been asked to keep this to eight minutes, and I want to say at the start that I intend to, because there is a sailing at eleven and I am not going to be the reason it goes late.

I want to say three things.
What this building is.
What it is not.
And what it cost, which is the part that usually gets left out of a speech like this and which I think you are entitled to hear.


First: what it is.

It is a shelter with a floor that does not flood, a roof that does not leak, sixteen seats, a working lavatory, and a covered walk to the pontoon that means you can get from a car to a boat without arriving wet.

That is the whole of it.
I want to be very plain about that, because there was a version of this project — there is always a version of this project — that had a café and a gallery and a viewing platform, and it cost four times as much and it would have opened in 2031.

We built the smaller thing.
I am not going to pretend that was a brave decision.
It was an affordable one.
But I have come to think it was also the right one, and I will tell you why.

For six years the people of North Landing have waited for the ferry standing up, in the open, on a piece of concrete with a bollard on it.
In winter they have waited in the dark.
Mothers with pushchairs have waited there.
Ada Halvorsen, who is eighty-one and who is sitting in the second row and who will be embarrassed that I have said so, has waited there twice a week for eleven years.

None of those people needed a gallery. They needed a roof.

There is a lesson in that which I would like the council to take away, and I say this in the friendliest possible spirit to the three councillors who are here: the thing people are waiting for is almost never the thing in the brochure.
It is the roof.
It is the lavatory.
It is the bicycle rack, which I notice has already got nine bicycles in it and which cost eleven hundred pounds, and which by my reckoning has done more for ridership than anything else on this site.


Second: what this building is not.

It is not a solution to the timetable.

I am aware — I am extremely aware — that there are people here this morning who would rather I had spent the last two years on the frequency of the Halloway service than on a building.
I have had letters.
Some of them were very well argued and one of them was in verse.

So let me say clearly what I cannot do from a platform.
The Halloway service runs hourly because we have one vessel that can work that crossing and because we cannot crew a second.
That is not a budget problem I have been hiding from you.
It is that there are not the people.
We have advertised for a master for Halloway four times in two years.
We have had six applicants.
We appointed one, and she left after nine months, for a job that paid better and did not require her to live at the end of a road that floods.

A terminal does not fix that.
Nothing I can build fixes that.
What might fix it is a pay settlement, a training berth, and about four years, and I am working on all three, and I am not going to stand here and promise you a result.

What I will promise is that you will hear the truth about it from me before you hear anything else from anybody.
That has been my practice for six years and I do not intend to change it this morning.


Third: what it cost, and who paid.

The building cost £486,000.
The district council contributed £300,000 from the coastal access fund.
The cooperative's members contributed £126,000, which is to say that four hundred and eleven people who use this service put their own money into a shelter for themselves and for people they will never meet. £60,000 came from the sale of the old Kestrel, which many of you will remember and some of you were sorry to see go.

It ran seven weeks late.
The reason it ran seven weeks late is that when we opened the ground we found services that were on no drawing anybody held, and we had to stop and find out what they were before we could put a wall through them.
That was nobody's fault and I am not going to invent a villain for it.

It ran £31,000 over.
That, I will take responsibility for.
We should have surveyed the ground before we fixed the price, and we did not, because surveying the ground costs eleven thousand pounds and at the time that felt like eleven thousand pounds we did not have.
It was, in the end, a very expensive economy.

I say that plainly because I would rather you heard it from me on the day the building opens than from an auditor in a paper in March.


I want to finish with something Ada said to me when we were arguing about the seats.

She wanted sixteen.
The design had twelve.
I said, reasonably I thought, that the busiest sailing carries eleven people from this landing and that twelve was therefore enough.

And she said: it is not for the eleven.
It is for the day when somebody's mother comes to visit.

I have thought about that a great deal since.
It is the best argument about public buildings I have ever been given, and it was given to me on a piece of concrete with a bollard on it, in the rain, by a woman who had been waiting twenty minutes for a boat.

There are sixteen seats.

The eleven o'clock sails in forty minutes.
There is tea inside, and I am told there is cake, and I have taken six of my eight minutes, which for me is practically abstinence.

Thank you all very much.


[Applause. The Chair declared the terminal open at 10.28.]
