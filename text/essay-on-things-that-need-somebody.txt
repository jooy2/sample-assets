THINGS THAT NEED SOMEBODY

An essay


There is a particular kind of grief that attaches to work being automated, and
it is almost never grief for the work.

I have been trying to name it for some years now, since a conversation with a
woman who had spent eleven years lighting the last gas lamps in a small
coastal town and who was, by the time I met her, six months into a better job
at a municipal depot. She was clear about the better job. She was clear that
the electric lights were superior in every measurable way. She was clear that
she would not have gone back if offered, and she said all of this without any
of the wistfulness the interview had been arranged to collect.

And then she said something that I have thought about ever since, which was
that the thing she missed was not the lamps. It was that the town had needed
somebody to climb the hill.

I want to take that seriously, because I think it is the whole of the matter
and I think we almost always get it wrong.


THE STANDARD ACCOUNT

The standard account of automation grief goes like this. A person does a job.
The job gives them income, structure, identity, and a place in a community. A
machine does the job instead. The person loses the income, and with it the
structure and the identity and the place, and is unhappy.

This account is not wrong. It describes a great deal of real suffering and it
underpins most of what is worth saying about redundancy, retraining, and the
distribution of the gains from productivity. I have no argument with it.

But it does not describe what the lamplighter was telling me, because she had
kept the income and the structure and, as far as I could tell, the place. Her
sister still made her soup. The depot foreman had told her she would do, which
in that part of the country is a testimonial. She had lost nothing the
standard account tracks.

What she had lost was a fact about the world: that at a certain hour, the town
was in a state that only her arrival could change.


NECESSITY AS A RELATION

I think what is at stake here is not the work but the relation the work
established, and that relation is necessity.

To be necessary is not the same as to be useful. A great many people are
useful without being necessary; that is what it means to have colleagues. Nor
is it the same as being valued, which is a judgement other people make and can
revise without your noticing. Necessity is a structural fact. It is true of
you whether or not anybody says so, and it is true in the specific sense that
if you do not act, the thing does not happen.

The lamplighter was necessary in exactly that sense, and she was necessary
every single evening, and the evidence of it was visible from every window in
the town. When she was late — and she was late once, badly, sitting on a step
reading a notebook her father had left in the base of a lamp post — the top of
the hill stayed dark.

Nobody, she told me, ever mentioned it. That detail matters. She was necessary
without being noticed, which turns out to be a stable and rather comfortable
arrangement, and it is not at all the same as being noticed without being
necessary, which is the condition a great deal of modern work has arranged for
itself.


THE SUBSTITUTION

Here is where I want to be careful, because there is a bad version of this
argument that I do not want to make.

The bad version says: the old ways gave meaning, the new ways take it, and
therefore the old ways were better. That argument has been made about the
plough, the loom, the telephone exchange, and the spreadsheet, and it has been
wrong every time, and it is wrong here. The electric lights are better lights.
The child who fell off the sea wall was found because of them.

What actually happened is subtler and worth stating exactly. The town's need
did not disappear. The town still needs the top of the hill lit. What changed
is that the need is now met by an arrangement rather than by a person.

An arrangement is a wonderful thing. It does not get tired, or ill, or twelve
minutes slower over four years without noticing. It does not have to be
recruited or trained or replaced when it retires. It is, in every respect that
can be put in a report, an improvement.

But it does not stand in a relation to anybody. The lights do not need the
person who typed the schedule into the machine forty miles away. If that
person leaves, someone else types it, and the lights do not know. The need has
been met so thoroughly that it has stopped being anybody's.


WHAT WE OFFER INSTEAD

When we notice this, we tend to offer three things, and none of them works.

The first is commemoration. We put up a plaque. The plaque in this town says
that gas lighting was maintained continuously until 2027 and records the
council's thanks. It does not name anybody, which the lamplighter told me she
was glad of, and I believe her, and I also think the reason it does not name
anybody is that a plaque cannot say the thing that would need saying. A plaque
can record that work was done. It cannot record that somebody was required.

The second is retraining, which is necessary and good and beside the point. A
better job is a better job. It does not restore a relation; it substitutes a
different one, and whether the new relation involves necessity is a separate
question that nobody asks during the retraining.

The third is the language of meaning. We tell people that their work had
meaning, and we mean it kindly, and it lands badly, because meaning is
something you are told and necessity is something that is true. The
lamplighter did not want to be told that her work had mattered. She knew that.
She wanted there to still be a hill that needed climbing.


THE PART I CANNOT RESOLVE

I want to end honestly rather than tidily.

I do not think there is a way to keep the necessity and the improvement both.
I have looked for one, in the way that people who write essays look for a
resolution that will let the last paragraph feel earned, and I have not found
it. If a task can be arranged for, it will be, and it should be, and the
person who used to be required will stop being required. That is not a failure
of policy. It is what an improvement is.

What I think we can do is smaller and less satisfying. We can stop pretending
that the thing being lost is the work. We can stop offering plaques and
retraining as though they addressed the same wound. And we can pay attention
to where necessity still lives, because it does still live in places, and it
is almost always somewhere nobody is measuring.

It lives in the person who notices that the child on the sea wall is too close
to the edge. It lives in the colleague who reads the file from 2011 before the
floor is opened. It lives in whoever, in a room full of arrangements, is the
one who will act if the arrangement fails.

Those people are necessary. They are almost never told so, and I suspect they
do not need telling, in the way the lamplighter did not need telling.

But it is worth knowing that the wound, when the arrangements finally reach
them, will not be about the work either.

lamplighter, and the conversation described in it are invented.
