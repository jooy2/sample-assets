ENID MARLOW

Parish clerk who saved a ferry with a piece of arithmetic

Born 4 February 1931; died 19 November 2011, aged 80


Enid Marlow, who has died at the age of eighty, was for thirty-four years the clerk to Halloway Bank parish council, a post she described in her only recorded interview as "mostly minutes, occasionally drains."

She held no other office, stood for nothing, and by her own account never spoke at a public meeting she had not been asked to.
Her name appears on no building and on no plaque.
She is remembered — by a small number of people, in a small part of the coast — because in the spring of 1994 she noticed that a column of figures was wrong, and said so, and would not be talked out of it.


The county council had proposed closing the Halloway ferry.
The case was straightforward and, on its face, unanswerable: the service carried 22,000 passengers a year at a subsidy of £31 each, which was eleven times the subsidy per passenger on the county's bus network and more than four times the next worst ferry.

Marlow, who was then sixty-three and had eleven months left before a retirement she had already announced, asked for the working.

She was sent it, in the way that a parish clerk is sent things, which is to say reluctantly and in a form not intended to be checked.
It ran to fourteen pages.
She worked through it over a fortnight at her kitchen table, on the back of an out-of-date timetable, in pencil, and found that the total annual cost included capital depreciation on the two vessels.

Both vessels were at that point thirty-one and twenty-eight years old.
Both had been fully depreciated in the accounts of the Fenwick and District Steam Packet Company, which had ceased to exist in 1971.
The county had inherited the boats, inherited a valuation, and gone on depreciating an asset whose cost nobody living had paid.

Removing the phantom depreciation reduced the subsidy per passenger by 61 per cent, to £12.09 — better than two of the county's own bus routes.

The proposal was withdrawn in July. The ferry is still running.


She was born Enid Cassell at Halloway Bank, the third of five children of a boatbuilder, and left the village school at fourteen.
She worked in the office of a net-making firm at Cooper Street until it closed in 1958, married Peter Marlow, a joiner, in 1959, and returned to the village where she had been born and where she remained for the rest of her life.

She became parish clerk in 1962, in the manner of the time: the previous clerk died, somebody suggested her, and nobody objected.
The post paid £84 a year.

The work was as she described it.
She took minutes at eleven meetings a year for thirty-four years — a total, as somebody worked out at her funeral, of 374 sets of minutes, every one of which survives, and which together constitute by some distance the fullest record of any settlement on that coast in the second half of the twentieth century.

Historians who have used them describe them as unusually complete and unusually dry.
She recorded what was decided, who dissented, and what the decision cost.
She did not record what was said.
Asked about this once, she said that a minute which recorded what people said would be read by the people who had said it, and would therefore change what they said, and would stop being a record.


Those who worked with her describe a person of almost aggressive ordinariness.
She dressed plainly, drove a succession of small cars badly, and gave up driving in 1994 on the grounds — as she put it to a neighbour — that she was sixty-three and could still tell.

She was not, by any account, a campaigner.
During the eleven years of the Halloway bridge inquiries she took no public position at all, and the parish minutes record only that the council resolved on four occasions to make no representation.
She thought the bridge a bad scheme; she also thought that a parish council with eleven hundred residents had no business spending its credibility on a decision that would be made elsewhere.

That reticence makes the 1994 intervention harder to explain rather than easier.
Those who asked her about it received a consistent answer, which was that she had not been campaigning then either.
She had been checking a number.

"They put it in a paper," she said.
"If you put a number in a paper somebody is entitled to add it up."


Her later years were quiet.
She resigned as clerk in 1996, as planned, on her sixty-fifth birthday, and declined a presentation.
She continued to attend meetings as a member of the public for a further nine years and continued, from the back of the room, to occasionally ask for the working.

Peter Marlow died in 2003. There were no children.

She spent her last five years increasingly housebound and was cared for at home by neighbours who organised a rota without telling her it was a rota, an arrangement she saw through immediately and permitted.

She is survived by two sisters.


The Halloway ferry now carries 30,615 passengers a quarter.
It recovers 68 per cent of its direct operating cost, which remains the worst figure of the four routes on the network, and every internal review since 1994 has recommended that it close.

It is still running.
The reviewers, it seems, keep being asked for the working.

There is no memorial to Enid Marlow at Halloway Bank.
This was her explicit instruction, given twice in writing, and the parish council — which she had taught, over thirty-four years, to do what the paperwork said — obeyed it.
