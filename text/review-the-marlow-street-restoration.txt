THE MARLOW STREET RESTORATION

A review

Fenwick District Libraries, Marlow Street branch
Reopened 4 August 2027


There is a particular sound that a well-made reading room has, and the Marlow Street branch has got it back.

It is not silence.
Silence is what a badly-made reading room has, and it is brittle, and everybody in it is conscious of it, and a cough goes off like a dropped tray.
What a good room has instead is a floor of soft noise — a radiator, a distant door, the particular rustle of a broadsheet being folded by somebody who has been folding broadsheets for sixty years — against which one reader can turn a page without eleven other people hearing it as an event.

Marlow Street had that sound until 2009, lost it to a suspended ceiling and a carpet, and has now, at a cost of £1.4 million and seven weeks of overrun, got it back.
Whatever else is said in this review, that should be said first, because it is the hardest thing in the brief and it is the thing the architects were least likely to be thanked for.


WHAT THEY DID

The building is an 1888 subscription library, given to the town in 1934, and altered five times since — twice sympathetically and three times not.

The restoration has reversed the three.
The suspended ceiling is gone and the original barrel vault is back, which recovers four metres of height and approximately half of the acoustic.
The 1970s mezzanine has been removed entirely.
The 2009 carpet has gone in favour of the original boards, sanded rather than replaced, with the scars left visible.

What is new: a lift, a second staircase, a lavatory that meets the current standard, and a glazed extension on the north side housing the children's collection and the public computers.

That is the whole intervention, and its restraint is the best thing about it.


WHAT WORKS

The vault.
Obviously the vault.
It was there the whole time, above a grid of tiles, and the decision to open it up consumed something like a third of the budget and produced the entire effect.
Everything else in the building is serving it.

The boards.
There was, apparently, an argument about the boards, and the conservation officer won it.
They are scuffed.
There is a worn track between the door and the issue desk, deep enough to feel through a shoe, cut by ninety-three years of people walking the shortest line between the two things they came for.
Sanding that flat would have cost nothing and destroyed the only piece of evidence in the building about how it was actually used.

The extension.
I did not expect to like it.
Glazed additions to Victorian municipal buildings are the default answer to a question nobody asked, and this one is not obviously different in kind.
What makes it work is that it is lower than the original eaves and set back from the street elevation, so that you cannot see it and the vault at the same time.
It is a separate building that happens to be attached, and the children in it are audible from the reading room at exactly the volume that tells you a library is being used and not at the volume that stops you reading.

The seats.
Sixty-two in the reading room, up from forty-four.
Somebody counted the queue.


WHAT DOES NOT

The issue desk is in the wrong place, and everyone who works there knows it.

It has been put where the original desk was, which is defensible on conservation grounds and wrong on every other.
The original desk served a subscription library where a member's business was concluded at the desk.
The present desk serves a service where most of the business is self-issue at a machine eleven metres away, and the effect is that staff spend the day walking between a desk they are meant to be at and a machine that is where the public actually needs help.

I asked about this.
I was told, with great politeness, that it had been raised.

The lighting is the second failure and the more serious one.
The vault is lit by uplighters that wash it beautifully and put almost nothing on the page.
Every table has a task lamp, and every task lamp has been positioned by whoever sat there last, and the result at four o'clock on a January afternoon — which I have now been to see — is a magnificent ceiling above a room of people angling lamps.

This is a real problem and it is not a small one.
A reading room that is difficult to read in has failed at the only thing it is named for.

The third is the lift, which is slow, and which announces each floor at a volume calibrated for a railway station.
There are two floors.


THE MONEY

£1.4 million, of which £900,000 came from the district, £380,000 from a heritage fund, and £120,000 from a public appeal that raised its target in nine weeks — a figure the committee should reflect on the next time it is told that nobody cares about libraries.

It ran seven weeks late.
The cause was a floor void containing services that were on no drawing the council held, which is a sentence that appears in the 2011 file about the same building and which nobody read before opening the floor.
That is not the contractor's failure and it is barely the council's; it is a filing failure, and it cost seven weeks and £31,000, and it will happen again in 2043 unless somebody puts the file somewhere obvious.


THE THING THAT IS NOT ON THE DRAWINGS

I want to finish with something the architects cannot take credit for and which is nonetheless the reason to go.

At half past nine on a Tuesday morning the doors open and about fifteen people come in, and they are not, for the most part, there for the books.
They are there because it is warm, and because it is free, and because it is the only room in the district you may sit in for four hours without buying anything or explaining yourself.

The restoration has made that room considerably better.
The vault is beautiful and the boards are honest and the seats are more numerous, and the children are audible in the right way, and none of it matters as much as the fact that the door opens at half past nine.

Fix the lighting. Move the desk. And then leave it alone for fifty years.


Marlow Street branch is open Monday to Saturday.
Admission is free, as it has been since 1934, which is worth writing down occasionally.
