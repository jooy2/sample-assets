WHY THE TIDE IS NOT WHERE YOU THINK IT IS

Lecture 4 of 12: Coastal Processes
Notes for students. These notes are complete; the lecture is not.


BEFORE WE START

Three warnings.

First, almost everything you were told about tides at school is a simplified
model, and the simplification is not innocent. It gets the cause roughly right
and the timing badly wrong, and the timing is the part anybody actually needs.

Second, I am going to spend most of this lecture on why the simple model fails
rather than on the model itself. If you want the model, it is in any textbook.
What is not in most textbooks is a clear account of the gap between the model
and the tide table on the harbour wall, and that gap is where the interesting
physics lives.

Third: none of this is a substitute for a tide table. If you take one thing
from today, take that. Every year somebody drowns on a causeway having
reasoned correctly from first principles about a coastline that does not care.


1. THE MODEL YOU WERE TAUGHT

The equilibrium theory of tides, which is what you got at school, says roughly
this.

The Moon's gravitational pull is stronger on the near side of the Earth than
on the far side. Water, being able to move, is drawn towards the Moon on the
near side. On the far side, the water is left behind — the solid Earth is
pulled away from it more strongly than it is. You get two bulges, one facing
the Moon and one directly opposite.

The Earth rotates beneath these bulges. Any given point passes through two of
them per rotation. Two high tides a day.

The Sun does the same thing at about 46 per cent of the strength. When Sun and
Moon line up, the effects add: spring tides. When they are at right angles,
they partly cancel: neap tides.

Every sentence in that account is correct, and the account as a whole predicts
almost nothing useful.


2. WHAT IT GETS WRONG

Take the equilibrium model seriously for a moment and ask it three questions.

Q: When is high water?
A: When the Moon is overhead, or directly underfoot.

Q: How big is the tidal range?
A: About 0.5 metres.

Q: Does it vary much from place to place?
A: Not really.

Now go and stand somewhere with a tide table.

At Fenwick the mean spring range is 6.2 metres — twelve times the model's
answer. High water occurs roughly five hours after the Moon's transit, not at
it. And forty miles along the same coast, at Kestrel Point, the range is 2.1
metres and high water is fifty minutes earlier.

The model is not slightly wrong. It is wrong by an order of magnitude in
amplitude, wrong by hours in phase, and it has no mechanism at all for
variation between two places on the same coast.


3. WHY IT IS WRONG: THE OCEAN IS NOT DEEP ENOUGH

Here is the single fact that unlocks everything.

For the equilibrium bulges to keep up with the Moon, the tidal wave would have
to travel westward around the Earth at the speed of the Earth's rotation —
about 1,670 km/h at the equator.

A shallow-water wave travels at the square root of (g × depth). For the
ocean's mean depth of about 4 km, that gives roughly 700 km/h.

The wave cannot keep up. It is not a bulge being dragged along; it is a wave
being driven at more than twice its natural speed, by a force that is
oscillating. That is a forced oscillation problem, and forced oscillations do
not sit obediently under the driver. They respond at the driving frequency but
with an amplitude and a phase set by the system's own properties.

This is the whole answer. Everything else is detail.

Write it down like this: the tide is not a bulge that the Moon drags round the
Earth. The tide is an oscillation of each ocean basin, driven at the frequency
the Moon supplies, with an amplitude and a phase determined by the basin.


4. WHAT THE BASIN DOES

An ocean basin has natural periods, the way a bathtub does and for the same
reason.

Slosh a bathtub at random and you get chop. Slosh it at its natural period and
you get a standing wave, and the water at one end goes up and down by far more
than the force you applied would suggest.

The semidiurnal driving period is 12 hours 25 minutes. Where a basin's natural
period is close to that, the response is large. Where it is not, the response
is small.

This is why the Mediterranean, which is large but whose geometry gives it
natural periods far from 12h 25m, has a tidal range of centimetres, while the
Bay of Fundy, whose natural period is very close indeed, has a range of over
fifteen metres.

Nothing about the Moon is different in those two places.


5. AMPHIDROMIC SYSTEMS

Add the Earth's rotation and the standing wave stops standing still.

The Coriolis effect deflects the moving water, and instead of a wave sloshing
back and forth along a basin, you get a wave rotating around a point. That
point is an amphidromic point: a place where the tidal range of that
constituent is zero, and around which high water travels like the hand of a
clock.

Consequences worth knowing:

- Tidal range increases with distance from the amphidromic point. Coastlines
far from one have big tides. - The time of high water advances progressively
along a coast, in the direction of the rotation. This is why high water at
Kestrel Point is fifty minutes before Fenwick and not at the same time. - In
the northern hemisphere the rotation is anticlockwise. In the southern, it is
clockwise. - There are several amphidromic points per ocean, and a different
set for each tidal constituent.

If you understand amphidromic systems you can look at a co-tidal chart and
predict, roughly, the shape of the tide anywhere on it. That is worth more
than memorising anything else in this lecture.


6. LOCAL AMPLIFICATION

Then the coast gets hold of it.

Three mechanisms, all of which apply on the estuary outside this window.

Shoaling. As depth decreases, wave speed decreases, and energy is conserved by
an increase in amplitude. A tidal wave entering a shelf sea gets taller.

Funnelling. A converging channel concentrates the same energy into a narrowing
width. The Fenwick estuary narrows from 14 km at the mouth to 900 m at Marlow
Point, which multiplies the range by roughly 2.4 before any other effect.

Resonance again, locally. An estuary of the right length for the tidal
wavelength resonates in its own right. The quarter-wavelength condition is the
one to remember: an estuary about a quarter of a tidal wavelength long, closed
at the head, will amplify strongly.

Fenwick is, unhelpfully for anyone living there, close to that condition.


7. THE SHAPE OF THE CURVE

One more thing that catches people out.

In deep water the tidal curve is close to a sinusoid: the rise and the fall
take the same time. In a shallow estuary it is not. Friction acts more
strongly on the low-water part of the cycle, so the flood becomes shorter and
steeper and the ebb becomes longer and gentler.

At Fenwick the flood takes 4 hours 50 minutes and the ebb takes 7 hours 35.

The practical consequence: the incoming tide moves faster than the outgoing
one. The rule of twelfths — that the tide rises 1/12, 2/12, 3/12, 3/12, 2/12,
1/12 of its range in the six hours — assumes a symmetric curve and is
consistently optimistic on a flood tide in a shallow estuary.

People cross causeways on the rule of twelfths. Do not.


8. WHAT THE TIDE TABLE ACTUALLY IS

A tide table is not a calculation from the Moon's position.

It is a harmonic prediction. For each port, the historic record is decomposed
into constituents — M2 (principal lunar semidiurnal, 12h 25m), S2 (principal
solar, 12h 00m), N2, K1, O1, and dozens more — and each constituent's
amplitude and phase at that port are measured from years of observation.

The prediction is then just the sum of those constituents projected forward.

Two things follow.

First, the table is only as good as the record it was built from. A port with
four years of data has a worse table than one with forty.

Second, the table predicts the astronomical tide only. It does not know about
weather. A deep depression raises sea level by roughly 1 cm per millibar of
pressure drop, and a sustained onshore wind can add far more. Storm surges of
a metre are unremarkable on this coast and two metres has happened twice in
living memory.

The table said the water would be at 4.1 m. The water was at 5.6 m. The table
was not wrong; it was answering a different question.


9. WHAT TO REMEMBER

If you remember five things:

1. The tide is a forced oscillation of ocean basins, not a bulge dragged round
   the Earth. The ocean is too shallow for the bulge model to work.

2. Amplitude and phase are set by basin geometry and resonance, not by the
   Moon's position. This is why two places on the same coast differ.

3. High water rotates around amphidromic points. Range grows with distance
   from them.

4. Estuaries amplify by shoaling, funnelling, and local resonance, and they
   distort the curve so that the flood is faster than the ebb.

5. A tide table is a harmonic prediction of the astronomical tide, and the
   weather is not in it.


READING

Any coastal oceanography text will cover sections 3 to 6 properly. For section
8, get hold of an actual harmonic constituent table for a port you know and
look at how much of the total is M2. On this coast it is about 71 per cent.
Somewhere with a strongly diurnal tide it can be under 20 per cent, and the
whole shape of the day is different as a result.
