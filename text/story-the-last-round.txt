THE LAST ROUND

A short story


The last gas lamp in Corrance stood at the top of Fisher's Rise, and on the evening the order came through, Mira Oduya climbed to it the way she had climbed to it every evening for eleven years: slowly, with the ladder over her shoulder and her father's brass key in her coat pocket, counting the steps out of habit rather than need.

There were ninety-four.
There had been ninety-four when she was nine years old and holding the ladder steady while her father went up, and there were ninety-four now, and she supposed there would be ninety-four tomorrow, when nobody climbed them at all.

The letter had come at noon.
It was very short.
The council thanked her for her service, noted that the electrical conversion of the Rise would be completed on the fourteenth, and enclosed a form regarding the return of council property.
The brass key was council property.
It had been council property for sixty years, which was longer than the council had existed under that name, and it had lived in a drawer in her family's kitchen for all of them.

At the top, she set the ladder against the post and did not immediately climb it.

The town lay below in the particular blue of an hour that has decided to become evening but has not yet committed to it.
Down at the harbour the new lights were already on — had been on since four, in fact, because the new lights did not wait for anything.
They came on at a time somebody had typed into a machine in an office forty miles away, and they went off at another such time, and between those two times they did not vary and did not fail and did not need anyone.

She had nothing against them.
That was the part she could never make her sister understand.
She had nothing against the new lights at all.
They were better lights.
They were brighter and cheaper and they did not go out in wind, and when a child fell off the sea wall two winters ago it was the new lights on the harbour road that let the doctor find her.

What she had was a key, and a ladder, and ninety-four steps, and no more evenings in which to use them.


She climbed.
The rungs took her weight the way they always had.
At the top she opened the little glass door and the smell came out at her — that particular smell of cold metal and old gas and the faint sweetness of the mantle — and she put the flame to it and watched the light take hold.

It was not a good light.
She was honest with herself about that, up there where nobody could hear her be honest.
It was yellow and it wavered and it lit a circle perhaps twenty feet across, and beyond that circle it did nothing at all except make the dark look darker.

But it was a light somebody had to come and make.
That was the whole of it, and she did not know how to say it to anyone without sounding sentimental, which she was not.
Every evening for sixty years, somebody had climbed Fisher's Rise because otherwise the top of the hill would be dark.
Somebody had been required.
The requirement had been the point.


Beatrix had made soup, which was what Beatrix did when she intended to say something difficult, and Mira had known before she took her coat off that the something was going to be about the letter.

"They're keeping two of the posts," Beatrix said, over the pot.
"Did you know that?
On Chandler's Walk.
Not lit — just standing.
For the look of it."

"I know."

"They're calling it heritage."

"I know what they're calling it."

Beatrix set the pot down harder than the pot required.
She was six years older and had spent all six of them being correct about things, and Mira had spent her own share of years learning that being correct and being right are not the same errand.

"You could apply for the depot," Beatrix said.
"Ada says they want somebody who knows the streets."

"I know the streets."

"So apply."

"I might."

The truth, which she did not say, was that she had already applied, four weeks ago, before the letter came, because she had known the letter was coming since March.
She had known since the surveyors were on the Rise with their tripods and their apologetic faces, and she had done the sensible thing immediately, the way she always did the sensible thing immediately, and then she had gone on climbing ninety-four steps every evening as if she had not.

The depot would have her.
She was fairly sure of that.
It was regular work and it was indoors and the pay was better, and there was no version of the next forty years in which she was right to refuse it.

What she could not explain to Beatrix, or to Ada, or to the polite young man from the council who had asked whether she wanted to be photographed for the newspaper, was that she was not grieving the job.
She was grieving a fact about the town: that until the fourteenth of this month, Corrance had needed a particular person to do a particular thing at a particular hour, and after the fourteenth it would need nobody, and it would be better off, and that was the part she could not hold in her head and her chest at the same time.


On the ninth she found the notebook.

It was in the base of the lamp post, in the little cavity behind the access plate where her father had kept a spare mantle and a stub of pencil and, evidently, this: a school exercise book, water-swollen, its cover gone, wedged upright so that it had stayed almost dry for what must have been thirty years.

The first page said, in handwriting she knew better than her own: Rise, times of lighting, from March.

It was a log.
Nothing more than that.
A date, a time, and occasionally a word — wind, or rain, or once, memorably, fox.
Her father had kept it for four years and then stopped, in the middle of a September, without any note explaining why.

She sat on the step at the base of the post and read all four years of it in the failing light, and what she found was not sentiment.
It was drift.
In the first March the lighting time was 6:40.
By the last September it was 6:52.
Twelve minutes, gained a minute or two at a time, across four years of a man getting slightly older and slightly slower up ninety-four steps and not once writing that down.

She thought about that for a long time.
She thought about the fact that the town had not noticed twelve minutes, and would not have noticed twenty, and that somewhere in the years after the notebook stopped her father had presumably gone on losing minutes until the evening he handed her the key and said nothing about it at all.

That was the first night she was late.
Not by twelve minutes; by nearly an hour, sitting on a cold step with a swollen notebook, while the top of Fisher's Rise stayed dark and nobody, so far as she could ever discover, remarked on it.


The conversion crew arrived at seven in the morning of the fourteenth and were finished by two, which offended her more than anything else about the whole business.

Seven hours.
Sixty years of evenings, and the replacing of it took seven hours and four men and a van, and one of the men ate a sandwich sitting on the step where she had read her father's notebook, and there was no reason on earth for him not to.

She had not intended to watch.
She had intended to be at the depot, where she had started on the second and where she was already, to her considerable irritation, quite good.
But the depot ran a half day on the fourteenth and she found herself walking up Fisher's Rise at half past two with no plan at all.

The foreman was a woman in her fifties with a clipboard and a manner Mira recognised from a lifetime of council employment, which was to say brisk and not unkind.

"You'll be the lamplighter," she said.

"I was."

"Mm."
The foreman looked up at the post, which now wore a grey fitting where the glass house had been.
"They usually want the old head.
As a keepsake.
It's yours if you want it; it only goes in the skip otherwise."

Mira looked at the lamp head, sitting on the tailgate of the van in the flat afternoon light.
Off, and in daylight, it was a very ordinary object.
Cast iron and four panes of glass, one of them cracked in a corner since before she was born.

"No," she said. "Thank you."

The foreman marked something on the clipboard and did not ask why, for which Mira was more grateful than she could have said.

She kept the key.

Nobody asked for it, in the end.
The form regarding the return of council property listed a ladder, a canister, and a set of tools, and it did not list a key, because whoever had written the form forty years ago had not thought of the key as a thing separate from the man who carried it.

She returned the ladder.
She returned the canister and the tools, and she signed the form, and she put the brass key back in the drawer in the kitchen, where it had lived for sixty years and where it lives still.


In November there was a storm, and the new lights on Fisher's Rise went out along with the new lights everywhere else, and for one evening the town was as dark as it had been in her grandmother's time.

Mira did not climb the Rise.
There was nothing at the top of it to climb for.
She sat at her kitchen table with a candle and listened to the wind and thought, without any particular satisfaction, that this was the night the argument would have been won if the argument had ever been about reliability, which it never had been.

The power came back at ten past nine.
She heard it arrive as a change in the quality of the dark outside the window, and she went to look, and the top of the Rise was lit, evenly and coldly and without anybody having gone up there.


In the spring the council put a plaque at the base of the post on Chandler's Walk — one of the two they had kept, for the look of it — and invited her to the unveiling, and she went, because Beatrix would have been unbearable otherwise.

The plaque said that gas lighting had come to Corrance in 1867 and had been maintained continuously until 2027, and that the council recorded its thanks to the lamplighters of the town.
It did not name anybody.
She was glad of that.
Her father would have been mortified to be named, and she would have been mortified on his behalf.

Afterwards a girl of about eleven asked her whether it was true that she used to light them all by hand, and she said yes, and the girl asked how long it took, and she said an hour and forty minutes on a good evening, and the girl said that was ages, and she agreed that it was ages.

"Weren't you bored?" the girl said.

"Every single evening," Mira said. "For eleven years."

The girl seemed satisfied by this, in the way children are satisfied by an adult telling them something true instead of something improving, and went to find her mother.


She still walks up Fisher's Rise sometimes, in the evening, when the weather is decent and the depot has not kept her late.
It is a good view and it is a hill she knows.

She does not count the steps any more.
But she knows there are ninety-four, and she knows that on a March evening in 1994 a man in his forties took eleven minutes to climb them, and that by September of 1998 he was taking twelve, and that nobody in the town below ever knew or needed to.

That is the thing she has decided to keep.
Not the lamp, and not the light, which was never a very good light.
Just the fact of the minutes, written down in a swollen exercise book by a man who had no idea he was writing anything at all.
