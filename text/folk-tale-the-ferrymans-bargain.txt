THE FERRYMAN'S BARGAIN

A tale of the Fenwick coast, as it is usually told


There was a ferryman once at the Halloway crossing, and his name was Corrin, and he was neither better nor worse than most men of his trade.

He had a boat that leaked in one place he knew about and one he did not.
He had a pole, an oar, and a lantern.
He had a rope on either bank and a bell on the Halloway side, because the bank there is high and a traveller standing on it cannot be seen from the water.

And he had a rule, which was his father's rule and his father's father's before that: that the last crossing of the day was made at dusk, and that after dusk the boat did not go out for any money in the world.

Now the reason for the rule is the part of the story that the tellers disagree about, and I will give you the three reasons and let you keep whichever suits you.

The first is that the estuary turns at dusk, and a man who does not know the turn will find himself two miles down and a mile out with the light gone.

The second is that a thing lives in the deep channel that does not trouble anyone in daylight.

And the third — which is the one my grandmother gave, and which I notice is the only one that has never needed anybody to believe in anything — is that a ferryman who will cross after dark will be asked to cross after dark every night of his life, and will die tired.


So.
It was the fourteenth of a wet October, and Corrin had pulled the boat up and turned the lantern down, and the bell rang on the Halloway side.

He did not go.

It rang again, and then a third time, and then it stopped, and Corrin ate his supper and went to bed and slept badly, which he would not have admitted to anyone.

In the morning there was a woman sitting on the Halloway bank with her back against the bell post, and she had been there all night.

She was not angry.
That is the part of the story that catches people out, and it is the part that must be told exactly, because a woman who was angry would be a different tale altogether.
She was not angry, and she did not reproach him, and when he brought the boat across she got into it and paid her penny and said good morning.

And when they were halfway over she said: "You have a rule, then."

"I have."

"It is a good rule," she said. "I would keep it too."

And Corrin, who had slept badly, and who had spent the whole of the night arguing with himself and losing, said the thing that undid him, which was: "There is not much I would break it for."

Which is not the same as saying there is nothing.

She was quiet the rest of the way.
When they touched the Fenwick bank she stepped out and turned round and said:

"Then here is a bargain.
I will give you one crossing.
One only, and yours to spend.
Ring the bell yourself on any night you like, and however the tide runs and however dark it is, you will come to the other bank safe.
But you may only use it once, and you may not use it for yourself."

And Corrin laughed, because he was a ferryman and not a fool, and said: "And what do you want for that?"

"Nothing," she said. "That is why it is a bargain and not a trade."

And she went up the road towards Fenwick and he never saw her again.


Now here is where the tellers part company for good, and I will give you all four endings, because the story is four hundred years old and has earned them.


THE FIRST ENDING

Corrin kept the crossing forty-one years and never spent it.

He was asked.
He was asked in the winter of the great fever when a child at Halloway was dying and the doctor was on the Fenwick side, and he took the boat out that night and made the crossing on his own skill in a rising tide, and got there and back, and the child lived, and he did not spend the bargain because he found he did not need it.

He was asked again when his own brother was on the wrong bank in a gale, and that night he did not go at all, and his brother walked the estuary road and arrived at dawn, wet and furious and alive.

And when Corrin was old and could no longer work the pole, he gave the boat and the rope and the lantern to a young man from Cooper Street, and he told him the rule about dusk, and he did not tell him about the bargain, because he had come to believe by then that it had never been a bargain at all: that she had given him nothing except the knowledge that he was the sort of man who would not spend it, and that this had been quite enough to be going on with.

He died in his bed. This ending is the one the schoolmasters like.


THE SECOND ENDING

Corrin spent it in the third year, on a stranger.

There was a man on the Halloway bank at midnight in November, and he was not important, and he was not dying, and he had no story that anybody afterwards could remember.
He had simply missed the last crossing, as people do, and he rang the bell, as people do, and he was going to have to sleep in the wet.

And Corrin, for no reason he could ever give, went out.

The tide was wrong and the dark was total and he made the crossing as though it were a bright afternoon in June, and he set the man on the Fenwick bank, and the man said thank you and went away up the road and was never heard of again in this story or in any other.

And in the morning Corrin found that the bargain was gone, and he sat on the bank for a long time and could not decide whether he had wasted it.

He never did decide.
This ending is the one my grandmother told, and when I asked her whether he had wasted it she said that was the question, and that a story which answered it would not be worth telling twice.


THE THIRD ENDING

Corrin spent it on himself, which he had been forbidden.

It was the night of the flood, and the water was over the Halloway road, and he was on the wrong side of his own crossing with his own house going under on the other, and he rang the bell and got into the boat and went.

And he arrived.
That is the thing the tellers always stress: he arrived, and his house stood, and he did not drown.

But the crossing took him eleven hours, and he came ashore on the Fenwick bank an old man, with the forty years he had not yet lived spent on the water in a single night, and he lived another two and could not remember, at the end, which bank he had started from.

This is the ending the old people tell, and they tell it in a particular voice, and it is not a voice that is enjoying itself.


THE FOURTH ENDING

There was no bargain.

There was a ferryman with a rule, and a woman who sat out a wet night against a bell post and was decent about it in the morning, and a remark that Corrin turned over for forty-one years because he had said more than he meant.

And everything that is said to have happened afterwards happened: the child in the fever year, the brother in the gale, the stranger in November who was let across at midnight for no reason at all.

He simply did all of it himself, on his own skill, in his own boat, and told nobody that there had been no help in it.

This is the ending I believe.
But I notice that it is also the only ending in which Corrin is entirely alone, and I notice that nobody at Halloway tells it, and I have come to think that a village keeps the version it needs and that this is not the same as keeping the version that is true.


THE BELL

The bell is still on the Halloway bank.
It is not the same bell; that one went in 1911 and there have been two since.
But it is on the same post, and the post is on the same footing, and the footing is four hundred years old and a different colour from everything around it.

The crossing is a ferry now, and it runs to a timetable, and the last sailing is at 17.05 in winter and there is no crossing after it for any money in the world.

Nobody rings the bell.
It is there for the look of it, and for the tourists, and because the parish would not survive taking it down.

But the ferry does not sail after dark on the Halloway route, and if you ask the operator why, you will be told about the tide and the channel and the crew rostering, and all of that is true.

And then, if it is late enough in the evening and you have bought the drinks, somebody will tell you it is because of Corrin, and they will not be joking, and they will not entirely be serious either, which is exactly the condition in which a four-hundred-year-old story stays alive.


— This tale is invented.
It is a sample asset, written for a repository of test material.
There is no Halloway crossing, no Corrin, and no bargain; the tradition it pretends to record does not exist.
