A MANIFESTO FOR A SLOWER TIMETABLE

Being eleven propositions on the running of public transport,
offered without authority by somebody who has waited a great deal


PREAMBLE

This is a manifesto, which means it is more certain than the evidence
strictly permits. I have tried to mark the places where I am guessing.

It is written by a passenger, not an operator. Operators will find at least
three of the propositions naive, and I have kept them anyway, because a
manifesto that has been through an operations review is a policy document and
the world has enough of those.

Everything here is about a small coastal ferry network, because that is what I
know. I believe most of it generalises. I would not stake much on the parts
about weather.


I. FREQUENCY IS A PROMISE; SPEED IS A DETAIL

A service that runs every twenty minutes does not need to be consulted. You
leave the house, you arrive, you wait an average of ten minutes, you go.

A service that runs every ninety minutes must be consulted, and the
consultation is the cost. Not the eighty minutes you might wait — the fact
that the journey has become a thing to be planned rather than a thing to be
done.

Every minute you shave off the crossing is worth something. Every minute you
shave off the average wait is worth roughly three times as much, and nobody
publishes it, because crossing time is in the brochure and waiting time is in
the passenger's afternoon.

Guessing here: the factor of three. I have seen it argued as high as five.


II. A TIMETABLE THAT IS CLEVER IS A TIMETABLE THAT IS WRONG

Tidal adjustments. Seasonal variants. A different pattern on Sundays. Three
sailings that run only in school terms.

Every one of these is defensible on its own and the sum of them is a service
nobody can hold in their head.

The test is simple and I have never met an operator who applies it: can a
regular passenger tell you, without looking, when the next one goes?

If not, the timetable is wrong, however efficient it is. Efficiency that
consumes the passenger's attention has moved the cost rather than removed it.


III. THE LAST SAILING IS THE WHOLE SERVICE

People do not choose a service by its first departure. They choose it by its
last.

If the last crossing is at 17.05, then the service is unusable by anyone who
works a normal day, and it does not matter that there are eleven crossings
before it. Those eleven crossings serve the people who were already going to
use it.

One late sailing changes who the service is for. Eleven extra
middle-of-the-day sailings change how comfortable the existing users are.

Operators reliably do the second, because the second is cheaper and the
numbers look better per pound. The numbers look better because the numbers
count journeys and not people.


IV. RELIABILITY IS NOT AN AVERAGE

Ninety-four per cent reliability sounds excellent and describes a service that
fails once a fortnight.

Once a fortnight is enough. It is enough for a passenger to conclude that the
service cannot be relied on for anything that matters, at which point they
acquire a car for the days it fails, at which point they use the car on the
days it does not.

The threshold is not a percentage, and it is not the same everywhere, and I do
not know what it is. What I am confident of is that it is a threshold and not
a slope: below it people plan around you, above it they plan without you, and
the transition is fast.


V. TELL PEOPLE EARLY AND ACCEPT THAT THEY WILL BE ANGRIER

A cancellation announced forty minutes ahead on a phone produces more anger
than the same cancellation discovered by standing on a pontoon in the rain.

I have heard this offered as an argument for announcing late. It is an
argument for announcing early and taking the anger.

When the weather does it to you, it is nobody's fault. When a message does it
to you, somebody decided. The second is worse to receive and better to live
with, because the person who received the message went home dry, and next
week they will still be a passenger.


VI. THE LAST MILE IS THE SERVICE

The strongest single predictor of ridership on the network I know is not the
vessel, the fare, or the crossing time.

It is whether the terminal has secure bicycle parking.

I did not believe this. I now believe it, on the operator's own data, and I
think the mechanism is obvious once stated: a ferry gets you across the water
and leaves you two miles from where you are going. Whoever solves the two
miles owns the journey.

An operator who spends a million pounds on a vessel and eleven hundred pounds
on a bicycle rack has misallocated by a factor I am not going to attempt.


VII. BUILD THE ROOF BEFORE THE CAFE

Everybody waiting is waiting for the same four things: to be dry, to be
seated, to know when, and to be able to use a lavatory.

There is no fifth thing. There is a very long list of things that appear in
architectural competitions, and the list is not the fifth thing; it is a
different list, produced by people who are not waiting.

Build the roof. Then build the seats. Then, if there is money, build the café,
and do not pretend the café was for the passengers.


VIII. COUNT THE SEATS FOR THE DAY SOMEBODY'S MOTHER VISITS

The busiest sailing carries eleven people. Twelve seats is therefore adequate
and sixteen is waste.

This reasoning is correct and it is the reason waiting rooms are unpleasant.

A public building is not sized for its mean load. It is sized so that on the
day it is unexpectedly full, nobody is standing. The four extra seats cost
almost nothing and are the difference between a room that accommodates you and
a room that has calculated you.


IX. FARE INTEGRATION BEATS EVERY TIMETABLE CHANGE

Passengers who pay twice behave as though the second leg costs more than it
does, because it does.

Every study of this that I have found — and I have found eleven — reaches the
same conclusion, which is that integrating fares between modes raises
ridership by more than any frequency change of comparable cost.

It is also the change least often made, because it requires two organisations
to agree and a timetable requires one.

This is the proposition I hold most strongly and it is the one entirely
outside any single operator's power, which I suspect is not a coincidence.


X. SUBSIDY PER PASSENGER IS THE WRONG NUMBER, AND EVERYONE KNOWS IT

We ask what a ferry route recovers. We do not ask what a road recovers.

This is not a debating point. It determines which services are reviewed, and a
service that is reviewed every year eventually fails a review.

If the comparison is to be made at all, it must be made against the
alternative that would actually be used, on the same basis, including the
alternative's capital and maintenance. Nobody does this, because doing it
takes a fortnight and asserting the recovery rate takes a line in a
spreadsheet.

Meanwhile a route that is the only crossing for a settlement with no
alternative is compared against a commuter route in a city, and loses, and the
comparison is repeated the following year.


XI. THE SERVICE IS THE FACT THAT SOMEBODY UNLOCKS THE DOOR

At the end of all of it: the network is not the vessels, and it is not the
terminals, and it is certainly not the app.

It is that at 06.15 somebody unlocks a gate and a boat goes, and that this
happens on the days when it is raining and on the days when eleven people want
it and on the days when three do.

Everything in this manifesto is in support of that. Where any proposition here
conflicts with it, the proposition is wrong.


A NOTE ON WHAT I HAVE NOT SAID

I have said nothing about decarbonisation, which matters more than anything on
this list and about which I know nothing useful.

I have said nothing about crewing, which is the binding constraint on every
service I know and which no manifesto fixes.

And I have said nothing about the possibility that a route should close. Some
should. A manifesto is a bad instrument for that question and I have not
pretended otherwise.


— This manifesto is a work of fiction written as a sample asset. The network
it describes, its data, and its author are invented, and none of the
propositions should be taken as advice about any real transport service.
