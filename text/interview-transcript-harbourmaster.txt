ORAL HISTORY PROJECT: WORKING THE FENWICK ESTUARY
Interview 14

Subject:    Aurelio Bassani, harbourmaster (retired)
Interviewer: Y. Beaumont
Recorded:   11 March 2027, at the subject's home, Cooper Street
Duration:   1 hour 47 minutes (extract)
Transcribed: 3 April 2027

Transcription conventions: [ ] editorial insertion; ... trailing off; (( )) non-verbal; --- interruption. The subject reviewed and approved this transcript on 20 April 2027.


YB:  Can I start by asking you to say who you are and what you did.

AB:  Aurelio Bassani. I was harbourmaster at Fenwick from 1996 to 2019, and before that I was assistant from '88, and before that I was on the boats.

YB:  On the boats from when?

AB:  Seventy-four. I was sixteen. ((laughs)) My mother was against it.

YB:  Why was she against it?

AB:  Because her father had been on the boats and she'd seen what it did to him. Which was fair. It was a different job then. You want me to talk about that or do you want the harbourmaster part?

YB:  Whatever you want to talk about.

AB:  Mm. Well. The difference is this. When I started, if the weather was bad, you went and you found out how bad. Now they know before they leave the berth. That's the whole difference and everything else follows from it.

YB:  Can you give me an example?

AB:  Nineteen seventy-nine. The February. We went out to the Kestrel bank on a forecast that said force five and it was force nine by the time we were round the point, and there was no way of knowing that except by being there. Eleven hours. We got in at four in the morning and the skipper -- he'd been forty years at it -- the skipper said, and I remember this, he said "Well, we know now."

     ((pause))

     That's it. That's the whole of the old way. We know now.

YB:  Were you frightened?

AB:  No. That's the thing nobody believes. I was sixteen and I was too stupid to be frightened and everybody older than me was too busy. You're frightened afterwards. You're frightened in about 1994, at three in the morning, out of nowhere.


YB:  Tell me about becoming harbourmaster.

AB:  It wasn't a promotion. People think it was a promotion.

YB:  What was it?

AB:  It was a different job that happened to be on the same water. On the boats you have one problem at a time and it's in front of you. As harbourmaster you have forty problems and none of them are in front of you, they're all in a radio or a form.

     The first year I was terrible at it.

YB:  In what way?

AB:  I kept going out. There'd be something happening in the harbour and my legs would take me down the steps, and by the time I got there whoever was dealing with it had dealt with it and now they had me to manage as well. ((laughs)) Took me about eighteen months to learn to stay in the office.

YB:  Who taught you that?

AB:  Nobody taught me. Marguerite told me. Not the same thing.

YB:  Marguerite ---

AB:  Oyelaran. She was at the libraries. We were on a committee together for about six years, the flood committee, and she said to me once -- we were waiting for something to start -- she said "You've got a boat problem and a desk job and you keep solving the boat problem."

     ((pause))

     I didn't like that. I thought about it for about a year and then I liked it.


YB:  What was the hardest part of the job?

AB:  Weather cancellations.

YB:  Not incidents?

AB:  No. Incidents are terrible but they're clear. Something's happened, you do the thing, you write it up, you don't sleep for a week, it's over.

     A cancellation is a decision. Nobody's dead. Nobody's going to be dead. You are standing in an office looking at a forecast and a tide and eleven people on a pontoon, and you are deciding whether they get home tonight.

     And you're wrong sometimes. Both ways.

YB:  Can you say more about being wrong?

AB:  ((long pause))

     There was a Thursday in November, I want to say 2004. Fog. I cancelled the last Halloway. Eleven people on the bank and I cancelled it and they went round by the road, which is fifty minutes, and one of them was a woman with a child who'd been at the hospital all day.

     And the fog lifted at twenty past six.

YB:  How did you feel?

AB:  How do you think? ((pause)) But that's the wrong question, if you don't mind me saying. The question isn't how I felt. The question is whether I'd do it again, and the answer is yes, every time, because at half past five I couldn't see the far bank and I don't get to know about twenty past six.

     You can only decide with what's in front of you. Everybody says that. Nobody believes it at half past five.

YB:  Did you tell her? The woman.

AB:  No.

YB:  Why not?

AB:  What would I have said? ((pause)) I think about that more than the decision, if I'm honest with you.


YB:  What changed most over your career?

AB:  Three things. Radar, phones, and the crewing.

     Radar's obvious. Phones -- I mean the fact that a passenger can ring the office. In 1988 if you cancelled a sailing, people found out by standing on a pontoon. By 2015 they found out on a phone forty minutes before, and they were much angrier, and I've never worked out why.

YB:  Why do you think?

AB:  Because when you're standing on a pontoon in the rain, the weather's doing it to you. When it's a message on a phone, I'm doing it to you.

     Same decision. Different villain.

YB:  And crewing?

AB:  Crewing's the thing that'll finish it, and nobody's listening.

     When I started there were four families in Fenwick that put people on boats, every generation, automatically. Nobody recruited them. They just turned up at sixteen like I did and somebody's uncle vouched for them.

     That's gone. It went in about 2005 and nobody noticed for ten years because the people who'd already turned up were still there.

     Now they advertise. They advertised four times for a Halloway master in two years and got six applicants. Six. In 1990 you'd have had sixty and forty of them would have been someone's cousin.

YB:  Is that a bad thing? The cousins?

AB:  ((laughs)) Now that's a good question.

     It was a closed shop and it kept people out who should have been in, and I'm not going to sit here and be sentimental about it. Some of those cousins were useless.

     But it worked. It put people on boats without anybody having to arrange it. And when you take away a thing that works badly, you have to put in a thing that works, and we took it away and put in nothing, and now there are six applicants.


YB:  What do you miss?

AB:  ((pause)) The half hour before the first sailing.

     I'd be in at half four, five. The office is cold. Nothing's happening anywhere. You put the kettle on and you look at the forecast and the tide and the boat list and for about half an hour the whole day is still possible.

     After the first sailing it's not possible any more, it's just actual.

YB:  Do you go down to the harbour now?

AB:  Every day. ((laughs)) Every single day, and I stand well back, and I don't say anything, and Nkechi -- she has the job now -- she comes out and says good morning and doesn't ask my opinion, which is exactly right and which took me about two years to appreciate.

YB:  Would you have wanted her to ask?

AB:  God, no. The worst thing you can have is the last one hanging about.

     ((pause))

     Although I'll tell you what I did do. I wrote her a letter. On the last day. Everything I knew that wasn't in the handover, about eight pages. Left it on the desk.

YB:  Did she ever say anything about it?

AB:  Not a word. ((laughs)) Not one word, in eight years.

     And then last March there was a bad night -- a real one, a proper one -- and she rang me at eleven o'clock and asked me one question, and it was a question that was on page six.

     ((pause))

     So she'd read it.

YB:  That must have meant something.

AB:  It meant she'd read it. That's all I wanted.
