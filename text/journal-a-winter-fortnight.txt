A WINTER FORTNIGHT

From the journal of a relief keeper
Gull Rock, January


4 January

Landed at 11.40, an hour late, in a swell that made the transfer more interesting than either of us wanted.
Renn had my bag on the ladder before I was properly on it, which I took as a compliment and which was probably impatience.

He was away on the same boat.
Nine minutes on the rock, the two of us, and in those nine minutes he told me: the second generator will not start when it is cold and there is a trick to it involving the throttle which he demonstrated too fast for me to follow; the radio has a dead spot when the wind is in the north-east; and there is a gull that comes to the kitchen window at four every afternoon and has done for two years and is not to be encouraged.

Then he was down the ladder and gone, and the boat went round the point, and that was that.

Fourteen days.
The relief is booked for the eighteenth, weather permitting, which out here is a phrase that does a great deal of work.


5 January

Woke at 5.50 without an alarm, which is my body's opinion and not mine.

Spent the morning on the inventory because it is the job I was given and because I wanted something with a beginning and an end.
Everything is present except a box of shackles that the list says are here and that are not, and a second-best torch, and eleven metres of the eighteen-millimetre line.

Wrote all three in the book.
It is a small thing and I have already thought about it more than it deserves, but I want the next person to find a book that is right, because I have been the next person.

Generator started first time. The trick may not be needed above four degrees.

The gull came at four.
I did not encourage it.
It stayed eleven minutes and then left in a manner I can only describe as disappointed.


6 January

Fog, all day, thick enough that I could not see the water from the gallery.

This is the thing nobody explains properly about a rock in fog.
It is not that you are alone.
You are alone on a clear day too, and on a clear day you can see the coast and the sailings and the light on the Kestrel Point road at six, and you are alone in the ordinary way that anybody in a house is alone.

In fog you are alone differently.
The world is not out there and hidden.
The world has been reduced to about thirty metres in every direction, and inside those thirty metres you are not alone at all: you are with the fog signal, which goes every ninety seconds, and after four hours it is going in your chest rather than in the air.

Read forty pages of a book about mountains, which I brought deliberately.


7 January

Clear and cold. Everything I could not see yesterday is back where it was.

Two sailings visible from the gallery.
The 09.00 and the 13.00.
The 17.00 must have gone round the other side.

Did the lamp maintenance properly for the first time — the cleaning, not just the check.
It takes two hours and eleven minutes, and the last forty of those are polishing glass that is already clean, and I understand exactly why the manual specifies it and I also understand why every keeper I have ever met does it in ninety.

I did the two hours and eleven.
There is nobody here to be impressed and I did it anyway, which I record because I think it is the first honest thing I have written in this journal.


8 January

The dead spot in the radio is real and is not the wind direction.
It is the time of day.
It is bad from about 15.30 and clears by 18.00, and it does this whichever way the wind is.

I mention this because Renn has been coming out here for six years and has built a theory about the wind, and the theory is wrong, and he will go on believing it because it has never cost him anything.

I will write it in the book. He will read it and not believe it.


9 January

Nothing happened. This is worth recording as such.

The temptation on a rock, I am discovering, is to make things happen so that the day has a shape.
You invent a job.
You decide the store needs reorganising or the paint on the east door needs looking at.
Then you do the invented job carefully, and at the end of it the day has a shape, and you have spent it on nothing.

I did not do that today.
I did the checks, I read, I cooked properly rather than out of a tin, and I sat on the gallery from four until it was too cold, and the day had no shape at all, and I think that is allowed.


10 January

Gale from the south-west. Force eight by the anemometer and it felt more.

You do not sleep in that.
Not because of fear — the rock has been here since 1847 and has an opinion about weather — but because the noise has no rhythm to it.
Rain on a roof you can sleep through.
Wind cannot make up its mind.

At about two in the morning something went over on the landing stage and I lay there for forty minutes deciding whether to go and look, and did not, and in the morning it was a crate that had been empty since before I arrived.


11 January

Cleared up.
Spent the morning on the landing stage checking what the gale had done, which was very little, and the afternoon writing a list of what would have gone if it had been force ten.

The relief is on the eighteenth.
Seven days.
I notice I have started counting, which I did not do in the first week.


12 January

The gull came back.
I had assumed it had gone for the winter and in fact it had only gone for the gale, which is more sense than I showed.

I did not encourage it.
I want that on the record.
What I did was eat my lunch by the window at four o'clock, which is a different thing, and which the gull and I both understood perfectly.


13 January

Radio contact with the mainland at 10.00 as scheduled.
Ines on the other end, who asked whether I was all right in a tone that suggested somebody had put her up to it.

I said I was, and I am.
But I understand the question.
There is a thing that happens to people on rocks, and the way it starts is not despair, it is routine: you stop doing the two-hour lamp clean, and then you stop cooking properly, and then one morning you do not get up at 5.50 because there is no reason to, and after that it is quite a short road.

I am doing the two hours and eleven minutes.
I intend to keep doing it until the boat comes.


14 January

Found Renn's list.

It was in the back of the store cupboard, behind the paint, in a plastic sleeve with tape gone yellow.
Six years of relief keepers, each one signed, each one with a line of comment.

Most of the lines are practical.
Generator trick.
Radio.
Store key.
But there are four that are not, and all four say roughly the same thing in different handwriting, which is: the first week is the difficult one, and if you get past the ninth day you will be sorry to leave.

I am on the eleventh day and I am already sorry, and I would very much like to know which of them wrote it first.


16 January

Missed a day. Not from anything; I simply did not write.

Weather is settled.
Two clear days, light airs, the coast visible from Halloway Bank all the way round to the Fenwick light.

Have done the inventory a second time and found the shackles.
They were in the wrong box, which is to say the box they have evidently been in since before the list was written, which means the list was wrong and not the store.
I have corrected the list.
It felt disproportionately satisfying and I am not going to examine why.


18 January

Boat at 09.20, early, because the weather is going tomorrow.

Renn came up the ladder looking pleased with himself and said, before anything else, "Wind in the north-east, is it," and I said no, it is the time of day, and I showed him the book, and he read it and said "Mm" and put it down and I know for a fact he does not believe me.

I have left the lamp cleaned properly, the store correct, and eleven days of notes about the radio.

I have signed his list.
My line says: the first week is the difficult one.
Which I now know is not original, and which I have written anyway, because it is true and because at some point in the next six years somebody is going to find that sleeve behind the paint on their fourth bad night and count how many of us said it.


— This journal is a work of fiction written as a sample asset.
Gull Rock, its light, and everyone in these entries are invented.
