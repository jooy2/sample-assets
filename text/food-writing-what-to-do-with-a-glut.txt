WHAT TO DO WITH A GLUT

On cooking too much of one thing


There is a particular despair that arrives in the second week of a glut, and it is not the despair of having too much.
It is the despair of having already made the obvious thing.

Anybody can deal with the first four pounds of runner beans.
You cook them.
They are the best runner beans of the year, because they are the first, and you eat them with butter and too much pepper and you feel that the whole business of growing food has been vindicated.

It is the twenty-second pound that is the problem, and the fact that there are another forty coming, and the fact that they are coming whether or not you have thought of anything.

I have been on the wrong end of a glut most years since I was thirty, in one crop or another, and I have arrived at four principles and about six recipes.
The principles are more use than the recipes.


PRINCIPLE ONE: STOP TRYING TO MAKE IT INTERESTING

The instinct is to search for variety.
A glut of courgettes sends people looking for eleven different courgette recipes, on the theory that the problem is monotony.

The problem is not monotony. The problem is volume.

Eleven different courgette recipes will consume, between them, about as many courgettes as one good one made three times, and each of the eleven will require something you do not have.
Meanwhile the courgettes are still arriving.

Find one preparation that consumes a lot, that you actually like, and that survives being eaten repeatedly.
Make it in quantity.
Make peace with it.

The test of whether you like something enough is not whether it is delicious.
It is whether you would eat it on a Tuesday without noticing.
Most delicious things fail that test.
Most useful ones pass it.


PRINCIPLE TWO: CHANGE THE STATE, NOT THE FLAVOUR

The reason a glut feels unmanageable is that the vegetable is in a form that occupies space and decays.

Everything you do to it should move it towards a form that does neither.
Cook it down, dry it, ferment it, freeze it, or turn it into something with a different clock.

Two kilograms of tomatoes fill a shelf and last four days.
The same tomatoes, roasted hard for two hours with garlic and olive oil and put into a jar under a layer of oil, occupy a third of the space and last a month, and are — this is the part people do not expect — better than they were.

The instinct is to reach for a recipe.
Reach for a state change first, and let the recipe wait until you know what you have got.


PRINCIPLE THREE: SALT IS NOT A FLAVOUR HERE, IT IS A TOOL

A glut is a preservation problem wearing the costume of a cooking problem.

Salt at 2 per cent of the weight of the vegetable, in a jar, under its own brine, at room temperature, for a week, and you have something that will keep for months and that tastes of itself and of time.

Cabbage is the famous one and it is the least interesting.
Runner beans do this beautifully.
So do green tomatoes, which otherwise have almost no use at all.
So, unexpectedly, do the woody stems of chard that everybody throws away.

Weigh the vegetable.
Multiply by 0.02.
That is the salt.
Everything else about fermentation is detail, and most of the detail on the internet exists to make a simple thing feel like an achievement.

The only rule that matters: the vegetable must stay under the liquid.
Whatever sits above the brine will spoil and whatever sits below it will not, and this has been true for about four thousand years.


PRINCIPLE FOUR: GIVE IT AWAY EARLY, NOT LATE

The moment to give away a glut is the moment you first suspect you have one, which is roughly a fortnight before you are certain.

Everybody waits.
Everybody gives away vegetables at the point of desperation, which is the point at which the vegetables are least good and the recipient most saturated, because everybody else's glut has arrived at the same time.

Give away the good ones in the first week.
You will be a person who gives away good vegetables, which has social consequences that arrive in October when somebody has too many apples.


THE SIX THINGS I ACTUALLY MAKE

Roasted tomatoes under oil.
Halve, cut side up, on a tray, salt, olive oil, a scattering of garlic.
140°C for two hours, until collapsed and slightly caught at the edges.
Into a jar, pressed down, covered with oil.
Refrigerate.
Uses about a kilo per tray and improves for a fortnight.

Brined beans.
Runner beans, topped, tailed, and cut into three; 2 per cent salt by weight; a bay leaf and a strip of lemon peel; weighted under the brine for eight days at room temperature, then cold.
Sour, salty, and completely unlike a cooked bean.
Good with anything fatty.

The courgette one.
Sliced thin, salted for half an hour, squeezed hard, then fried in far more olive oil than seems decent with a great deal of garlic and a long pause off the heat with vinegar and mint.
Consumes an alarming quantity and is better cold on the second day.

Green tomato and mustard seed.
Not a chutney, which takes a day and a great deal of sugar.
Green tomatoes in wedges, salted overnight, drained, then packed with mustard seed, dill, and a 3 per cent brine.
Ten days.
Sharp enough to cut through a plate of anything.

Chard stem gratin.
The part everybody discards.
Blanched four minutes, laid in a dish, covered in a thin béchamel and too much cheese, baked until brown.
It is a poor gratin by the standards of gratins and it is the best possible use for a thing that was going in the bin.

Soup, thin.
Whatever the glut is, softened in butter with an onion, covered in light stock, simmered, and blended half.
Thin, not thick.
The instinct is to thicken and the instinct is wrong: a thick soup concentrates a vegetable you already have too much of, and a thin one lets you eat two bowls.


A NOTE ON FREEZING, WHICH I HAVE BEEN AVOIDING

Freezing is the honest answer to a glut and I have written two thousand words avoiding it, which tells you something.

It works. It requires no skill. It is what any reasonable person does.

The reason I avoid it is that a freezer is a way of not deciding, and a glut is mostly a decision problem.
Bags go in and are not labelled and are found in March, and by March nobody knows whether the grey thing is beans or the other beans.

If you freeze — and you should — do the two things nobody does.
Label with the date and the quantity, not just the contents.
And write on the door what is inside, because you will not open a freezer to browse and you will therefore never find anything you have to look for.


THE ACTUAL POINT

I have come to think that a glut is not a problem with the garden.
It is the garden telling you the truth about how food used to work.

A vegetable has a fortnight.
That is the fact.
Everything else — the year-round courgette, the February tomato — is a distribution system built at enormous expense to conceal it from you, and it does conceal it, extremely well, until you grow eleven plants and it stops.

The despair in the second week is not really about the beans.
It is the recognition that you have been living inside an arrangement, and that the arrangement is very good, and that you have just stepped outside it and are now personally responsible for forty pounds of vegetables that do not care about your plans.

Weigh them. Multiply by 0.02. It has worked for four thousand years.


— This piece is a work of fiction written as a sample asset.
The quantities and methods described are plausible and untested; treat them as prose rather than as instructions.
