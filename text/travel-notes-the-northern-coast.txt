NOTES FROM THE NORTHERN COAST

Eleven days on foot between Fenwick and Gull Rock


DAY ONE — FENWICK TO MARLOW STREET

You are supposed to start at the quay, and I did, and it was a mistake.

The quay is where the ferries are and where the guidebooks tell you the coast path begins, and there is a waymarker there with an acorn on it and a queue of people photographing it.
What the guidebooks do not say is that the first four miles out of Fenwick are a road, and not a scenic one, and that if you start at the quay you will spend your first morning walking past a builders' merchant.

Start instead at the Marlow Street bridge and walk the estuary path west.
You lose the acorn and the photograph and you gain the entire estuary at low tide, which is the thing you came for.

I did not know this on day one.
I know it now because a woman with a dog told me at mile three, in the tone of somebody who has told a great many people at mile three.

Eleven miles.
Slept at the Cooper Street hostel.
Bunk room, four of us, and the man in the bunk below mine had walked from the other end and told me, with complete confidence, that the best day was day seven.
He was wrong, but he was wrong for an interesting reason, which I will come to.


DAY TWO — MARLOW STREET TO HALLOWAY BANK

The estuary at low tide is not a view.
It is a surface, and it is doing something.

At Fenwick the tide runs out over about four miles of mud and sand, and the whole of it is worked — by birds, by the water finding its channels again, by a handful of people in waders who are digging for something they were not inclined to discuss.
Nothing about it is still.
You walk beside it for six miles and it is a different colour every time you look up.

The far bank, from here, is a rumour.

At Halloway Bank the path turns inland for a mile and a half to get round the creek, which everybody complains about and which is the best mile and a half of the day: a sunken lane with hedges over the top of it, dark and green and about four degrees cooler than the coast, and you come out of it onto the bank at a gate that frames the whole estuary in one go.

Thirteen miles.
Stayed at the pub, which has four rooms and one of them is over the kitchen and you do not want it.
I had it.


DAY THREE — HALLOWAY BANK TO KESTREL POINT

Rain from ten until four.

I record this because a walking book that only describes good weather is a catalogue and not an account, and because the coast in rain is not a lesser version of the coast.
It is a different place with the same map.

Everything closes down to about two hundred metres.
The sound changes — the sea goes quiet under rain, which surprised me the first time and which I now know is a real acoustic effect and not an impression.
The path becomes a stream.
You stop looking at the view, because there isn't one, and you start looking at what is within two metres of you, which for six hours was: thrift, sea campion, one adder that was as surprised as I was, and about four hundred snails on a single stretch of wall.

I would not choose it. I would not have missed it either.

Fifteen miles, and the last four in a headwind that made them feel like six.


DAY FOUR — REST DAY AT KESTREL POINT

The guidebook does not schedule a rest day. Schedule one.

Kestrel Point is a village of about three hundred people arranged in a single street that runs down to a slipway, and there is nothing to do there, and that is the entire recommendation.
I sat on a wall for two hours and watched the 09.00 ferry come in and the 09.30 go out and then a gap of nothing until eleven.

The thing about a rest day on a long walk is not the rest.
It is that you stop being a person who is going somewhere.
For three days I had been converting the coast into distance.
On the fourth I stopped, and the coast stopped being distance, and the difference was startling and took about four hours to arrive.


DAY FIVE — KESTREL POINT TO NORTH LANDING

The cliff section, and the reason people do this walk.

Four miles of it, between about two hundred and three hundred feet, with the path close enough to the edge to be interesting and far enough back to be sensible.
There are three places where it is neither and you will know them when you reach them.

The birds are the thing.
I am not a birdwatcher and I had assumed the mentions in the guidebooks were the usual padding.
They are not.
There is a stretch of about eight hundred metres where the cliff face is occupied at every available ledge and the noise is continuous and the smell is considerable, and you can stand at the top and look down onto the backs of birds in flight, which I had never done and which rearranged something.

At North Landing there is a new ferry terminal, opened last month, and I want to say something about it because it is not what I expected.

It is a shelter.
That is all: sixteen seats, a roof, a lavatory, a covered walk.
No café, no gallery, no viewing platform.
I arrived at it wet and sat in it for forty minutes waiting out a squall, and in that time four other people came in and did the same, and one of them was a woman of about eighty who had clearly been waiting for boats at that spot for a very long time.

I have been in a great many pieces of coastal architecture that cost ten times as much and did less.


DAY SIX — NORTH LANDING TO THE CROSSING

A short day, deliberately, because the crossing only runs four times and you do not want to be running for it.

The path here leaves the coast for the only time on the whole route and goes through farmland, which the purists dislike.
The purists are wrong.
Nine miles of arable and hedge is exactly what you need after the cliffs, and there is a church at the top of the rise with the door open and nobody in it, and I sat in it for half an hour for no reason I can defend.

Crossed on the 16.05.


DAY SEVEN — THE CROSSING TO CHANDLER'S WALK

This is the day the man in the bunk told me was the best, and he was wrong, and here is the interesting reason.

Day seven is the famous one.
It has the arch, and the stack, and the beach that appears in every photograph of this coast, and it is genuinely spectacular.
It is also the only day on which you will meet more than about ten people, because there is a car park at either end and everybody who has one day walks this one.

It is spectacular and it is not yours.
On day three I saw four people in eight hours.
On day seven I saw, I would guess, two hundred, and every one of them was having a lovely time, and the coast had become a venue.

I am not superior about this.
I was one of the two hundred.
But if somebody tells you day seven is the best day, ask how many days they walked.

Fourteen miles.


DAY EIGHT — CHANDLER'S WALK TO TERN ISLAND CAUSEWAY

Read the tide table. Read it twice, and read the one for this year.

The causeway is passable for about four and a half hours either side of low water, and the posts are set on the safe line, and every year somebody decides the posts are being over-cautious.
The refuge box halfway across has a visitors' book in it and I recommend reading it.
It is not a cheerful document.

I crossed with three hours in hand and did not enjoy any of them.
It is a mile and a half of sand that is not sand, over which you can see the water sitting on both sides at a level that your legs do not believe.

Tern Island itself is four houses, a chapel, and a population of eleven.


DAY NINE — TERN ISLAND

Weathered in.

The causeway did not open all day — a combination of a big tide and a wind holding the water in — and so I did not walk, and there was nothing whatever to do about it.

This is worth planning for.
Everybody who walks this route budgets eleven days and eleven days is exactly right if nothing goes wrong, which means eleven days is wrong.
Budget thirteen.
The island has one place to stay and it was, that night, extremely full of people who had budgeted eleven.


DAY TEN — TERN ISLAND TO GULL ROCK LANDING

Out at 06.40 on a falling tide, in the dark, with a head torch and the posts appearing one at a time about ten metres ahead.

I would not do it again and I am glad I did it once.

The last stretch of the walk is a long shallow bay with the light on Gull Rock visible from about mile four, and it does not get closer for a very long time, in the way that a thing across water does not.
Eleven miles of walking towards a lighthouse that stays the same size.


DAY ELEVEN — GULL ROCK

You cannot land on the rock.
I had known this from the start and had somehow not believed it.

The boat goes round it — twenty minutes, twice a day in season — and you see the landing stage and the ladder and the door, and the keeper on the gallery raised a hand as we went past, and that was the end of the walk.

I had expected to feel cheated.
What I felt instead was that the rock was exactly the right ending, because the whole point of eleven days on this coast is that it is a place where the land runs out gradually and then stops, and a lighthouse you cannot land on is that fact with a building on it.


PRACTICALITIES

Distance: 118 miles as walked, which is nine more than the guidebook, because of the Halloway creek diversion and because I got the Kestrel Point path wrong.

Time: eleven days walking, thirteen elapsed. Budget thirteen.

Tides: essential on day eight and day ten.
Get the current year's table.
The one in the hostel is from 2019 and somebody will tell you it is close enough.
It is not close enough.

Where to start: Marlow Street bridge, not the quay.
I cannot stress this enough.

Best day: day three, in the rain, with two hundred metres of visibility and four hundred snails.
I accept that this is not a recommendation anybody can act on.


— These notes are a work of fiction written as a sample asset.
The coast path, the villages, and the walk described here are invented.
