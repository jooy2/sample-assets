THE ESTUARY IN FOUR SEASONS

Notes from a single mile of shore


A NOTE ON METHOD

For four years I have walked the same mile of the Fenwick estuary, between the sea wall at Cooper's Yard and the mussel poles, at low water, once a week, and written down what was there.

This is not science.
I have no transects, no counts that would survive being checked, and a bias towards the interesting that I have not corrected for.
What I have instead is repetition: two hundred and eleven walks over the same ground, which is enough to notice the difference between a thing that happens and a thing that happens every year.

The mile is not special.
That is rather the point.
It is mud, sand, a stretch of saltmarsh at the top, and a sea wall with a concrete apron.
There is nothing on it that would bring anybody from anywhere.


WINTER

The estuary in January is loud, and nobody expects that.

It is loud because it is full.
Thirty thousand birds use these flats between November and February, and although they are spread over four miles of it, they do not stay spread.
At the top of the tide they are pushed into the last uncovered ground, and the last uncovered ground on my mile is a bank about eighty metres long, and for about ninety minutes it is one of the densest things I have ever stood near.

Knot are the ones that make the noise.
Not calls — wings.
Four thousand knot lifting at once from eighty metres away is a sound like a wave breaking that does not stop breaking, and it goes on for perhaps eight seconds, and every time it happens I stop walking.

They do this because something disturbed them, and about one time in four the something is a peregrine.
The other three times it is nothing I can see, and I have come to accept that a flock knows things I do not.

Also in winter, in order of how often I see them: dunlin (constantly, in thousands, and I cannot count them and have stopped pretending), curlew (nine or eleven on the marsh, the same birds I think), redshank (noisy, first to go up, the flock's alarm system), brent geese (a hundred and forty in the best year, twelve in the worst), and shelduck, which are always exactly where the channel turns and never anywhere else.

The mud in winter is grey and smells of nothing.
This surprised me for the first two years.
Mud smells in summer.


SPRING

Spring on an estuary is defined by absence, which makes it the hardest season to write about and the most interesting to walk.

Through March the winter birds leave.
Not all at once: the brent go first, and then the knot in a fortnight around the end of the month, and then one week in April you arrive and the flats are empty and the noise is gone and it is extremely strange.

What is left is a handful of residents and a great deal of light.

The saltmarsh at the top of the mile does everything in about five weeks.
Sea-purslane greys over and then greens.
Thrift comes up in the second week of May in a quantity that would be photographed constantly if it were anywhere accessible.
Sea aster puts up leaves and no flowers and waits five months.

The other spring event is the invertebrates, and this is the part I understood last and which changed how I see the whole place.

For three years I walked past mud.
In the fourth year I dug a spade's depth of it and put it through a sieve, which took forty minutes and which I recommend to anybody, and the sample from one spadeful of my perfectly ordinary mile contained: eleven ragworm, about sixty Hydrobia — the tiny snail that the whole estuary runs on — four Corophium, and one small bivalve I could not identify.

That is a spadeful.
Extrapolated over the flats, badly, on a Sunday, with a calculator, it comes to a number I do not believe and have checked twice.

The birds are not visiting the mud.
They are visiting the animals in the mud, and the mud is not the setting, it is the larder, and once you have seen the sieve you cannot walk past it in the old way again.


SUMMER

Summer is the season with the fewest birds and the most life, and it is the season the estuary is least interesting to look at and most interesting to stand in.

The mud smells now.
It is a specific smell, sulphurous and organic, and it is the smell of anaerobic decomposition a few centimetres down, and it is a sign that the system is working extremely hard.

The tide comes over warm.
This is not a metaphor.
On a flood tide on a hot afternoon the first water over the flats has been sitting on sun-heated sand for six hours and it is genuinely warm, and there is a fifteen-minute window when the leading edge crosses my mile and the temperature difference between the front and the water behind it is several degrees.

Summer wildlife on the mile:

Shelduck with young, in July, in a crèche — one or two adults with twenty or thirty ducklings from several broods, which is a thing they do and which took me two years to stop misinterpreting as a very tired parent.

Sea-lavender on the marsh in late July, which is the one genuinely spectacular event of the year and lasts about eleven days.

Sandwich terns, offshore, hunting the channel on the ebb.
Never on the flood.
I do not know why and neither, as far as I can find out, does anybody I have asked.

And a great many small brown moths in the marsh grass that I have never identified and have decided not to.


AUTUMN

Autumn starts in July, which is the single most useful thing I have learned in four years.

The first returning waders arrive in the last week of July, when it is unambiguously summer, and they are adults that have failed to breed or have finished early, and they arrive in twos and fours and are easy to miss.
By late August there are hundreds.
By late September there are thousands.

The estuary year, in other words, has a winter of about eight months and a gap of about six weeks, and calling the seasons by their names on land has been actively misleading me the whole time.

The other autumn thing is the weather, and this is where the mile stops being gentle.

In September and October the first real gales come in from the north-east, and they arrive on top of the equinoctial spring tides, and the combination puts water over the sea wall about twice a year.
I have been there for it once, at distance, and it was a great deal more water moving a great deal faster than anything I had built a picture of from two hundred quiet walks.

Afterwards the mile is rearranged.
The channel had moved eleven metres north after the 2024 gale and has stayed there.
The mussel poles lost their western line.
The bank where the knot roost was two metres lower for a season and then, over eighteen months, came back.

That is the thing four years has taught me that one year could not: this place is not a scene.
It is a process running slowly enough to look like a scene, and a gale is simply the process going at a speed the eye can follow.


WHAT I WOULD TELL SOMEBODY STARTING

Walk the same mile. Not different miles.

Everything I have written here comes from repetition, and none of it would have been visible in four years of walking four different places.
The interesting observations are all differences, and you cannot see a difference the first time.

Go at low water.
The estuary is a car park at high tide and a country at low tide, and it takes about eleven visits to stop feeling that the exposed mud is the estuary being absent.

Dig one spadeful and sieve it. Once. It reorganises everything.

And write it down on the day, badly, in a notebook you do not care about.
I have four years of notes and about a third of them are in a form I can use, and all of the useful ones are the ones written standing up, in pencil, in the wind, in handwriting I can barely read.


— These notes are a work of fiction written as a sample asset.
The Fenwick estuary and the mile described here are invented, and the observations and figures in them should not be used as a record of any real place.
The natural history is generic and stated in the author's own words.
