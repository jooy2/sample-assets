THE BRIDGE THAT WAS NOT BUILT

Forty years after the Halloway crossing scheme was abandoned, the arguments against it have quietly become the arguments for the ferry


By R. Almeida
2 September 2027


There is a drawer in the Fenwick District Council archive, third floor, second cabinet from the window, that contains four hundred and eleven documents about a bridge that does not exist.

Planning applications.
Objections.
Two full public inquiries.
A geological survey of the Halloway bank so thorough that the ferry cooperative still uses it.
Eleven years of correspondence, three changes of government, one change of route, and a final letter dated 14 March 1987 confirming that the scheme would not proceed.

Nobody has looked in the drawer for a decade.
I have now spent three weeks in it, and what I found was not a story about a bridge.
It was a story about how completely a place can win an argument without ever noticing that it did.


THE SCHEME

The proposal, in its final form, was for a 1.2-kilometre crossing from Marlow Point to Halloway Bank, carrying two lanes and a footway, with an approach viaduct on the western side and a cutting through the ridge on the eastern.

It would have replaced a ferry that then ran six times a day and a road diversion of fifty minutes.
The traffic case was not marginal.
Even the objectors accepted that the bridge would have carried the traffic.

The cost, in 1985 prices, was £41 million.
The council's share would have been £6.2 million, which was rather more than the council's annual capital budget, and this was the first of the four things that killed it.


THE FOUR THINGS

The second was the ground.

The geological survey — the one the ferry cooperative still uses — found that the eastern abutment would sit on eleven metres of soft estuarine deposit over rock.
That is a solvable engineering problem and it was solved, on paper, twice.
Each solution cost money.
The 1985 figure of £41 million was the third figure; the first, in 1979, had been £23 million.

The third was the birds.

This is the one that everybody remembers and almost everybody misremembers.
The Halloway flats are a feeding ground, and the approach viaduct crossed the northern edge of them, and there was an objection on that basis, and it is routinely described in local memory as the reason the bridge was stopped.

It was not.
Read the inspector's report from the second inquiry — it is in the drawer, all 214 pages of it — and the ecological objection is dealt with in eleven paragraphs and dismissed.
The inspector accepted mitigation.

What the inspector did not accept was the fourth thing, which nobody remembers at all.


THE FOURTH THING

The fourth thing was that the bridge did not go where people went.

The scheme's traffic case was built on a 1977 survey of movements across the estuary.
That survey found, correctly, that 71 per cent of crossings were between Fenwick and the Halloway peninsula.
What it did not examine — because nobody asked it to — was where on the peninsula.

By the time of the second inquiry, in 1984, the county council had produced a more detailed study, and it found something awkward.
Two-thirds of those crossings ended within a mile and a half of the ferry slipway at Halloway Bank.
Only a fifth continued to the villages further out, which is where the bridge's eastern approach road actually went.

In other words: the bridge would have delivered most of its traffic to a point two miles beyond where that traffic wanted to be, and then required it to drive back.

The inspector's report puts it drily.
"The proposal appears to have been designed for the geography of the crossing rather than the geography of the journeys."

That sentence, more than the birds and more than the ground, is why there is no bridge.


WHAT HAPPENED INSTEAD

Nothing, for eleven years.

The ferry went on running six times a day with two vessels of increasing age, and the road diversion went on taking fifty minutes, and the peninsula went on losing about 1.4 per cent of its population every year.
In 1994 the county proposed closing the ferry altogether on the grounds that it carried too few people to justify the subsidy — a proposal which, read now, is startling for how nearly it succeeded.

What saved it was a piece of arithmetic that a parish clerk named Enid Marlow did on the back of a timetable.

She noticed that the subsidy per passenger was calculated on the ferry's total annual cost divided by its total annual passengers, and that the total annual cost included the two vessels' capital depreciation, and that the vessels were by then thirty-one and twenty-eight years old and had been fully written down in the accounts of a body that no longer existed.

Correcting for that, the subsidy per passenger fell by 61 per cent.

The proposal was withdrawn.
Marlow, who died in 2011, is not commemorated anywhere.


THE ARGUMENTS CHANGE SIDES

Here is the part that sent me back to the drawer a second time.

The objectors' case in 1984 rested on three propositions.
That a fixed crossing would concentrate development at one point on the peninsula.
That it would draw journeys onto the road network that the road network could not take.
And that the traffic forecast was built on a survey too coarse to support it.

All three were dismissed or discounted by the promoters.
All three are now council policy.

The current local plan's settlement strategy is explicitly against concentrated development on the peninsula.
The 2024 transport assessment says the eastern approach corridor — which is the road the bridge would have fed — is at capacity.
And the council's own guidance on transport modelling now requires origin-and-destination data at a resolution that the 1977 survey does not remotely meet.

Nobody framed any of this as a retrospective vindication.
I do not think anybody involved was aware of the connection at all.
The 1984 arguments simply became, over forty years, the ordinary assumptions of the people writing the plans.


THE FERRY NOW

The Halloway service runs hourly, seven days, from 08.05 to 17.05, with a seasonal evening extension.
It carried 30,615 passengers in the quarter to 30 June.
It recovers 68 per cent of its direct operating cost, which is the worst figure of the cooperative's four routes and has been for six years.

Every internal review recommends closing it. It is still running.

I put the recovery figure to the cooperative's chair, expecting a defence.
What I got instead was a question.

"What does the eastern approach road recover?"

The answer, of course, is nothing, because roads are not asked.
That comparison is made often by ferry operators and it is usually a debating point.
In this case it is more than that, because the road it is being compared with is the road the bridge would have built.


WHAT THE DRAWER DOES NOT SETTLE

I want to be careful here, because there is a tidy version of this article that I do not believe.

The tidy version says: they were right to stop it, the ferry is better, the peninsula was saved.
That is not what the documents show.

The peninsula was not saved.
It has lost 31 per cent of its population since 1977 and the school at Halloway Bank closed in 2016.
There is no serious argument that the ferry has prevented that, and there is a reasonable argument — made at the time, and made in the drawer, by people who lost — that a fixed crossing would have slowed it.

What the documents show is narrower.
They show that the bridge as proposed would not have done what its promoters said, that the objectors identified this correctly, that the identification was made on evidence rather than sentiment, and that the place has since organised itself around the objectors' analysis without ever crediting it.

Whether a different bridge, to a different point, would have been better than forty years of an hourly ferry is a question the drawer does not answer, and nobody has asked it since 1987.


Somebody should.
