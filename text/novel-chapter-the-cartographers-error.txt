THE CARTOGRAPHER'S ERROR

Chapter Eleven


The error was on sheet forty-one, and it had been there since 1911, and Devraj Pillai found it on a Tuesday afternoon in a room with no windows because he was avoiding a meeting.

That is the honest account and it is the one he gave, eventually, to the inquiry.
He had not been looking for it.
He had been looking for somewhere to be for ninety minutes, and the map room was cold and unpopular and nobody ever went there, and he had pulled sheet forty-one off the rack for no better reason than that it was the one nearest the light.

Fenwick estuary, 1:10,560, surveyed 1909, published 1911.
Third edition, with revisions to the railway.

He had seen it before.
Everyone in the department had seen it before; a copy hung in the corridor outside the director's office, badly framed, sun-bleached along the western third.
What Devraj had never done, in four years, was put it flat on a table next to the current sheet and look at both at once.

The causeway was in the wrong place.

Not badly wrong.
Not a mile out, or a hundred metres, or anything that a surveyor would have noticed on the ground.
On the 1911 sheet the causeway to Tern Island ran from the point at Chandler's Head across the flats on a bearing that was — he measured it three times with a rule, and then a fourth time with the digital caliper he had to go and fetch, which meant leaving the room and risking the meeting — eleven degrees south of where it ran on the modern sheet.

Eleven degrees, over a mile and a half, is about three hundred metres at the far end.

He stood there for a while and thought about the ways this could be nothing.

The first way was that the causeway had moved.
Causeways do not move, but the posts that mark them are replaced, and a village that replaces posts over a century might drift the line without anyone deciding to.
That was easy to check and he checked it that afternoon: the parish records at Halloway Bank listed every post replacement since 1888, with a bearing, and the bearing had not changed since the list began.

The second way was that the 1911 survey was simply wrong.
This was the comfortable answer and it was the one the director gave him a fortnight later, in the meeting he had by then been unable to avoid.
Old surveys contain errors.
Everybody knows this.
An eleven-degree error in a minor feature on a coastal sheet was, the director said, "not evidence of anything except 1909."

Devraj had been ready for that.

He had spent the fortnight in the archive, and what he had found was that the 1909 surveyors had been extremely good.
He had checked eleven other features on sheet forty-one against modern positions: the church at Halloway Bank, the two mussel poles that survive, the corner of the sea wall, the boundary stone at Cooper's Yard, the crooked marker, the slipway, four field corners, and the Fenwick light.

Every one of them was within four metres.

"Four metres," he said, in the meeting, and put the sheet on the table.

"And the causeway is three hundred out.
The same surveyors.
The same afternoon, probably.
They did not have an eleven-degree error in them."

The director looked at the sheet for what Devraj remembered afterwards as an uncomfortably long time.

"Then what," she said, "are you telling me?"

And Devraj, who had rehearsed this on the train and had a careful version prepared, heard himself say the uncareful one instead.

"I am telling you that in 1909 the causeway was where they drew it."


The trouble with that sentence — and he understood the trouble as soon as it was out of his mouth — is that it is not a statement about a map.
It is a statement about a mile and a half of sand.

If the causeway had run eleven degrees further south in 1909, then the safe line across the flats had been eleven degrees further south, and the safe line across the flats is not a decision anybody makes.
It is a property of the sand.
It is where the bank is highest and where the channels do not cut across.
You do not choose it; you find it, and then you mark it, and the marks are a record of the finding.

Which meant one of two things had happened.

Either the sand had changed — a mile and a half of it, three hundred metres at the far end, in a hundred and sixteen years — and nobody had written it down.

Or the marks were wrong, and had been wrong for a century, and everybody who crossed the causeway to Tern Island was walking a line that had been the safe line in 1909 and had stopped being it at some point nobody could name.

The second possibility was the one that made the room go quiet.


He did not sleep much that week. Not from drama; from arithmetic.

The causeway carries, at a rough estimate, four hundred crossings a year.
It is passable four and a half hours either side of low water.
It has a refuge box at the halfway point with a visitors' book in it, and Devraj had read the visitors' book, because he read everything, and it is not a cheerful document.

The entries fall into two kinds.
The first kind is people who misread the tide table and got caught, and there are eleven of those since 1994, and every one of them says the same thing in different handwriting, which is that the water came from the wrong direction.

The second kind — there are four — say something else.
They say that the posts ran out.

He had read those four entries a dozen times.
The last was from 2019.
A family of three, crossing in the afternoon, who had followed the posts as far as the bend and then found, in their words, "no more posts for about eighty yards" and had gone back, and had made it, and had been very frightened, and had written about it in the box on the way home because they did not know what else to do with it.

The parish had investigated.
The parish had walked the line the following week at low water and found every post standing.

Of course they had.
They had walked it at low water.
The family had crossed at half tide, when the water was already in the low ground on the southern side, and had been looking for posts on a line that no longer had any, because the posts they could see were three hundred metres to the north and behind them.


He put all of this in a note.
Four pages, no adjectives, every claim sourced.
He sent it to the director on a Thursday and he sent a copy to the parish clerk at Halloway Bank on the same day, which was the thing he was later criticised for and the thing he would do again.

The director's reply came on the Monday and was three lines long.
It said that the department would commission a survey of the causeway line, that the survey would take four months, and that in the meantime no statement would be made.

The parish clerk's reply came on the Friday afternoon, forty hours after he sent it, and said:

We have closed the causeway.
There is a notice at both ends.
We will keep it closed until somebody with a theodolite tells us where it is.

Please come and show us the map.


He went on the Saturday.

There were nine of them in the parish room, which he later learned was most of the adult population of Tern Island and about a third of Halloway Bank, and they had put out chairs, and somebody had made tea, and on the table at the front they had laid the framed copy from the church, which was the same third edition and which nobody had looked at properly in fifty years either.

He put his sheet next to it. He put the modern sheet next to that.

He did not have to explain anything.
A woman of about eighty in the second row said "That's not right" within about four seconds, and pointed, and she was pointing at exactly the place.

"No," said Devraj. "It isn't."

"It's gone north," she said.

"Yes."

"Since when?"

And that was the question, and he had come all this way to say the only honest answer, which was that he did not know, that nobody knew, that a hundred and sixteen years of a mile and a half of sand had gone unrecorded because a causeway is not a thing anybody surveys, and that the only reason he knew it at all was that he had been avoiding a meeting.

He said all of that, and it took about two minutes, and when he stopped there was a silence of a kind he had never encountered in a professional setting and has not encountered since.

Then the woman in the second row said:

"Well. That's more than anyone's told us before."
