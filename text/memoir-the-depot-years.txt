THE DEPOT YEARS

From a memoir


I was forty-one when I stopped lighting lamps and started at the depot, and for about a month I thought I had made a mistake, and the mistake I thought I had made was not the one I had actually made.

The one I thought I had made was leaving.
The one I had actually made was assuming that the new job was the same kind of thing as the old one, only indoors.

It is not.
I want to write down the difference, because nobody told me and because I have since watched three other people arrive at that depot from outdoor work and go through exactly the same four weeks.


The lamplighting had a shape.
That is the whole of it, and everything else is elaboration.

I started at the harbour end at a time that varied with the season and I finished at the top of Fisher's Rise, and in between there were forty-one lamps in an order that had been fixed before I was born.
When the last one was lit, the work was over.
Not paused.
Over.
There was no more of it anywhere, and I walked home past lamps I had lit, which is a kind of receipt.

The depot does not finish.

Things arrive at it.
A pallet, a fault report, a driver with a question, a delivery that is wrong.
Between the things arriving there is a state I did not have a word for in my first month and which I now know is the actual job: being available for the next thing.

That is a skill.
I did not know it was a skill.
I thought it was the absence of a skill, and I spent four weeks feeling that I was being paid for nothing, and in those four weeks I did what everybody does, which is invent work.

I reorganised the small parts shelving.
I did it on a Thursday, without being asked, because it was there and it was wrong.
On the Friday the foreman looked at it for a long moment and said, "You'll do," and walked off.

I have thought about that sentence for years.
At the time I took it as approval of the shelving.
It was not.
She had watched me spend a whole shift on a job nobody needed, in order to make the day have a shape, and she had recognised the disease and diagnosed it as survivable.


The second thing nobody tells you is about being new at forty-one.

I had been the best in the district at something for eleven years.
Nobody said so, because nobody says that about lamplighting, but I knew it: I knew the wind on every corner of that town and which mantles went early and which post had a stiff door in the cold.
Eleven years of knowing things.

At the depot I knew nothing.
I did not know where the returns went.
I did not know that you never put a pallet in bay four because the shutter fouls it.
I did not know that Vesna in the office needs the paperwork by three or she cannot close, and that she will not tell you this, and that if you are late she will simply stay until half past five.

I was, for about six months, the least useful person in the building, and I was forty-one, and both of those facts were true at once and neither of them was anybody's fault.

Here is what I did about it, and I offer it as the only advice in this chapter: I wrote things down.

Not to remember them.
I remembered them fine.
I wrote them down because writing them down turned not-knowing into a project, and a project is something you can be competent at while you are being incompetent at everything else.

I still have the notebook.
The first page says: bay 4, shutter.
The second page says: Vesna, three o'clock.
The eleventh page says: it is all right to ask Aurelio twice, he does not remember telling you.


The third thing is about the people, and it is the part I got most wrong.

I had assumed — I would not have admitted this at the time — that a depot was a lesser sort of place than a hill with a lamp on it.
That the work was transactional, that nobody would care much, that I was going somewhere ordinary after somewhere that mattered.

Within a year I had revised that entirely, and the thing that revised it was a Tuesday in February when the road flooded.

Nobody organised what happened next.
There was no plan and no meeting.
What happened was that Casimir, who had a van, went and got the two drivers who lived the wrong side of the water, and Nkechi rang round every delivery on the day's list and moved eleven of them without asking anybody's permission, and Vesna stayed until nine, and the foreman spent the entire afternoon on the phone being pleasant to people who were being unpleasant to her.

At six she came out and said, to nobody in particular, "Right, that's the day," and went home.

That is the same thing as ninety-four steps.
It is exactly the same thing.
It is a group of people doing a job that had to be done, at a particular hour, because otherwise it would not have been done.
I had been looking for it in the shape of my own day and it was in the shape of theirs, and it had been there the whole time.


I want to be honest about what I lost, because a chapter that says it all worked out is a chapter that is lying.

I lost the receipt.
There is no evening at the depot when I walk home past the work and see it lit.
There is no moment when it is finished.
There is a shift that ends and a shift that begins, and the difference between a good day and a bad one is legible to about four people, none of whom will mention it.

I lost being necessary in the specific, visible, evening way.
That went on the fourteenth of that September and it is not coming back and I have stopped expecting it to.

And I lost — this is the smallest one and the one that took longest — the walk.
An hour and forty minutes, alone, outdoors, six days a week, for eleven years, thinking about nothing.
I did not know that was a thing I was getting until I was not getting it, and I have never found a substitute, and I no longer look for one.


What I gained is harder to write down, which I think is the point.

I gained a room full of people who will move eleven deliveries without being asked.
I gained a job I can still do at sixty.
I gained the entire experience of being new at something and coming through it, which I had not had since I was nineteen and which I would recommend to anybody who is beginning to think they know things.

And I gained, eventually, the small parts shelving, which is still in the order I put it in on that Thursday, and which four people have since told me is much better, and which I now understand the foreman let me spend a whole day on because she knew exactly what I was doing and thought I needed it.

She was right. I did.


— This chapter is a work of fiction written as a sample asset.
The town, the depot, and everyone in it are invented.
